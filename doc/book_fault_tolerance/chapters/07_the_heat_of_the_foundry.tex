\chapter{The Heat of the Foundry}

Outside the corrugated iron walls, a dry November wind came down off the northern ridge and rattled the high louvers. Every leaf in the coppice woods had dropped two weeks back, leaving bare gray alder branches against a low sky. Inside the primary bay of the Foundry, however, it was over a hundred and ten degrees on the lower catwalk.

Elara hooked one boot toe behind the angle-iron upright of Furnace Three's scaffolding, leaned back into her climbing harness, and pushed her smoked-glass visor up onto her helmet. Sweat stung the corner of her left eye, cutting clean tracks through the black foundry dust on her cheeks. Below her, three colossal black-brick induction pots radiated a dry, shimmering heat that smelled of hot silica, graphite grease, and scorched wool.

"Give that collar bolt another quarter-turn, Silas!" she shouted over the sixty-cycle thrum of the preheat coils. "If the retaining ring creeps at peak draw, we split the crucible liner!"

Ten feet down the truss, clipped to the main stanchion in a scorched leather apron, Silas braced both boots against the cross-tie and leaned his weight onto a three-foot breaker bar. Rusted threads shrieked twice before seating against the lock washer.

"Seated on the witness mark!" Silas called up, wiping his forehead on the canvas upper of his sleeve. Orange light from the lower vent slots lit the underside of his chin and cheekbones. "Collar's tight. How's your lead?"

"Bolted!" Elara tightened the brass lug on the braided copper ground strap, tugged it twice with a gloved fist, and climbed up onto the catwalk grating. Unclipping her lanyard, she leaned against the pipe rail and drank lukewarm water that tasted of iron straight from her canteen.

Silas pulled himself onto the grating a moment later, unhooked his harness, and leaned his elbows on the rail beside her.

"Battery house says we've got a ninety-minute window at full draw," he said, looking down into the open throat of Furnace Three where three tons of sheared county truck axles and rusted bridge plate lay stacked like cordwood. "Solar's dropping behind cloud cover on the south ridge. If this melt drags out..."

"It won't drag," Elara said, screwing the cap back onto her canteen, though her stomach tightened around the warm water.

Fourteen hillside farms and three machine shops down the creek basin had shifts scheduled around this pour. Local carbon scrap like truck axles made fine structural billets for Sector Four's wind-tower frames and mill shafts, freeing the smaller shops to run surgical lathes and pump housings, or letting Master Vera's electronics benches keep clean voltage for circuit work, without every hamlet burning power on its own tiny forge. For high-impact turbine runners or rock drills, they still relied on vanadium-chromium alloy ingots traded down from the Iron Creek mines in the northern highlands, whose autumn pack train wasn't due until after the bioregional assembly; today's structural carbon steel had to get Sector Four's wind towers bolted up before the ground froze solid.

"Sector Four's crane crew goes idle at dawn if those bearing billets aren't cooling on the flatcars tonight," Elara said.

Silas rested his heavy leather glove over hers on the pipe rail for two seconds, tapped once, and let go.

"Then let's go melt some axles," he said.

They climbed down the iron ladder to the main floor. Elara stepped up to the analog control desk, a steel cabinet of phase meters, rheostat wheels, and hardwired current gauges, while Silas took the floor post beside the hydraulic tilt levers. Around the sand pit, eighteen foundry hands in aluminized coats and face shields checked their skimming rakes and hooked dry sand molds onto the narrow-gauge rail cars. Whistle blasts and hand signals carried orders across the bay where voices couldn't reach.

"Substation confirms bus is locked," Elara called into the wired horn mic. "Closing main contactor in three, two, one."

She pulled the knife-switch lever down into its copper-jawed catch.

The floor plates buzzed right through the soles of her boots. Heavy copper coils buried inside Furnace Three's refractory brick drank four hundred kilowatts at once, tightening the air until loose iron filings on the concrete danced upright in tiny bristles.

"Four hundred Celsius," Elara read off the optical pyrometer. "Seven-fifty. Nine hundred."

Through the thick quartz viewing port, the tangled heap of old bridge plate blushed dull plum, brightened to cherry, and began slumping inward as the alternating magnetic field churned the softening steel from the core out.

"Phase two is drifting!" Elara barked as the middle ammeter needle began slapping violently against its right-hand pin.

Silas didn't wait for a second call. Snatching the eight-foot insulated grounding lance off its wall hooks, he ran across the concrete apron toward the lower bus box. If coil two stayed out of phase under full load, magnetic torque would shear the anchor bolts straight out of the masonry and spill white-hot metal across the pit.

He drove the copper spike of the lance hard into the earth-shunt socket at the base of the housing. Fat blue arcs spat across his boot toes with a sharp crackle of ozone.

"Dropping!" Elara shouted, spinning the heavy cast-iron rheostat wheel counterclockwise with both hands to trim capacitance on bank two. "Hold it on the shunt, ten more seconds!"

Silas kept his boots planted wide on the rubber mat, leaning forward into the vibrating fiberglass shaft until his arms shook. Gradually the violent slap of the ammeter needle narrowed, steadied, and settled dead center alongside banks one and three.

"In phase! Latching!" Elara slammed the mechanical detent home.

Silas yanked the lance clear, stepped back out of the heat wash, and lifted two fingers through the smoke.

Inside the pot, the pyrometer crossed fifteen hundred and forty degrees Celsius. Rusted bridge girders and truck axles were gone; in their place rolled a blinding yellow-white bath of liquid steel.

"Skimmers up!" Elara called.

Four senior apprentices stepped to the refractory lip with long iron rakes. Floating atop the clean metal was a crust of blistering orange slag made of old lead highway paint, rust scale, and road grit boiled up out of the scrap. Working in pairs, they dragged the glowing crust over the slag notch into dry sand buggies, stepping back every two minutes so the next pair could take the heat.

When the last crust cleared, the bath shone flat and bright as mercury. Tarn, a fifty-year-old patternmaker whose gray eyebrows had been singed short a dozen times over, dipped a long spoon sampler, quenched the button on the test anvil, and shot it with the handheld spectrometer.

"Point-four-two carbon, sulfur under point-zero-three!" Tarn shouted, holding a thumb high. "Right on the print!"

A ragged yell went up from the rail pit.

"Pull the locking pins!" Elara called. "Silas, bring her over slow!"

Silas eased both hydraulic levers back half an inch. On greased trunnions, the massive black drum tilted forward.

Liquid steel spilled over the ceramic spout in a white-hot ribbon that lit the soot-blackened roof trusses like midday sun. Even twenty feet back at the control desk, the radiant heat hit Elara's exposed neck like an open oven door. One sand flask after another rolled beneath the stream on iron wheels as Silas feathered the valves, filling each mold cleanly to the riser cup while jets of dry steam hissed from the vent holes.

As the twelfth and final mold topped off, Silas eased the levers upright. Furnace Three settled back onto its stops with a dull clunk, its empty throat glowing deep tangerine.

For ten seconds nobody moved except the roof exhaust fans spinning up overhead.

Silas walked up the three iron steps to the control platform. Soot blackened the creases around his eyes, and a fresh spark burn smoked faintly on the cuff of his coat. Without a word he caught Elara by both elbows, pulled her against his chest, and rested his chin hard against the crown of her helmet.

Salt, hot wool, and coal smoke filled her nose. Elara gripped the scorched canvas behind his shoulder blades, feeling the rapid hammer of his pulse through two layers of heavy duck cloth. Neither of them moved back as fast as they usually did; for five full breaths she kept her forehead pressed against the brass snap at his collar before stepping half a pace clear.

"Twelve clean billets," she said, her voice rough from smoke.

"Twelve," Silas said. "Sector Four gets their wind towers."

\bigskip

Half an hour later, with their heavy aluminized coats hung on the drying pegs and the night crew banking the furnace brick for slow cool-down, the day shift sprawled along the wooden benches of the air-cooled washroom, passing salted mint tea and cold well water down the line.

Silas tossed Elara a wet aluminum canteen. Her fingers fumbled the catch once before closing around the cold metal, and she drank half of it before coming up for air.

"Cleanest carbon curve we've hit since August," Tarn said, scrubbing black grit off the back of his neck with a wet rag. "Thought bank two was going to throw a shoe there for a minute, though."

"Wait ten seconds longer on that shunt and we'd be chipping frozen steel out with jackhammers all week," Elara said, sitting down beside Silas and letting her knee rest against his.

"Man's getting spry in his forties," Tarn said gravely.

Silas snorted into his canteen and didn't bother answering.

At the end of the bench, a young runner from the northern workshops looked up from his slate. "Message just came across from Master Vera's bay," he said. "After today's microwave spoof on the roof relay, she's asking if we can bump Tuesday's high-purity silicon melt ahead of the second structural steel batch so she can pot thirty backup current clamps."

Elara set her canteen between her boots and ran the calendar in her head. "Bump silicon to Tuesday and we push the lower bridge-girder pour into next weekend," she said. "The Highland pack train with our turbine alloy isn't even down the pass yet, and if a November freeze locks the creek road before Sector Four finishes pouring concrete pads for those wind towers, we lose a third of our winter wind margin."

"Vera's worried they'll try spoofing the hydro house next," the runner offered.

"Hardwire the hydro house off the radio bridge tonight, same way she locked Furnace Two," Silas said, leaning his forearms on his thighs. "Sensors don't heat cabins when the sun goes behind the ridge for six weeks. Towers go up first."

Heads nodded along the benches. Elara pulled her slate off her belt, typed a three-line schedule note authorizing Vera's bay to draw six spare industrial clamps out of the emergency mill locker in the meantime, and signed it.

Outside the washroom windows, dusk had turned blue-black, and the first scatter of sleet ticked dryly against the iron siding. Beside her on the bench, Silas tipped his head back against the concrete wall and shut his eyes while the overhead fans hummed steadily on.
