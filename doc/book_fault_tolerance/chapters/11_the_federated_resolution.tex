\chapter{The Federated Resolution}

Four days of sleet and two thousand pairs of boots had churned the roadside pasture at South Fork into gray freezing soup that pulled rubber overshoes clean off people's heels.

Along a half-mile shoulder where the valley logging road met Route Nine stood forty-two canvas wall tents, six converted steel shipping containers, and three hoop-houses skinned in double-wall polycarbonate. Smoke from wet alder splits hung low over the mud alongside the sour reek of city tire-soot steaming off wet coats inside the drying sheds.

Elara stood ankle-deep in slush outside Intake Container Two, waving a family of four wrapped in foil emergency blankets toward the soup hatch while checking wrist tags on her slate. Her fingers inside greased leather mittens ached up to the wrists.

Overhead, a porcelain cutout on Pole Four cracked like a pistol shot and spat blue-white sparks into the sleet.

"Silas!" Elara shouted as twenty exhausted arrivals under the soup awning ducked at once.

Forty feet up the creosote timber pole in spikes and a leather belt, Silas was already reaching across the cross-arm with long-handled insulated shears. Pushed forty percent past nameplate rating to feed the camp's resistance heaters, the salvaged municipal pole-pig was humming high and shrill. Sparks danced off Silas's rubber lineman sleeves as he clipped the blistered lead, wrenched the lug clear, and clamped a heavy braided copper jumper onto the secondary bushing. Immediately the arcing died back to a strained sixty-cycle drone. Silas leaned back against his pole strap for two breaths, steam rising off his wet flannel collar into the sleet.

Elara pulled back her coat cuff. Her forearm display didn't blink; it sat solid red across the top banner:

\textit{LOCAL FLOOR WARNING: VALLEY GRAIN AND BATTERY RESERVES AT 46 HOURS TO AUTOMATIC CUTOUT.}

And the wider regional board looked uglier than Tuesday night.

Since the midnight breakup at the sawmill, the cracks across the bioregion had hardened in three different directions. Up on the Northern Plateau, five wealthy orchard and cattle hamlets hadn't bothered trying to repeal their sanctuary floor, knowing they could never win an eighty-percent household vote. Instead, Tuesday morning their clerks had inflated local soil-cover and winter hay reserve requirements by forty percent on paper so the shared formula reported zero exportable surplus, then posted \textit{No-Transit} markers across the plateau ridge road to block refugee vans. Yesterday afternoon, two regional watershed auditors had matched rainfall gauges and August barn tallies against the doctored entries and flagged the bad-faith numbers: automatic sanctions had already throttled the plateau's trade priority to the bottom of every workshop queue. Worse for the orchard bosses, nineteen of their own younger nurses, mechanics, and pruning hands, furious after watching shivering families with coughing toddlers turned back at the ridge gate in the sleet, had packed their personal tool rolls, invoked their individual right of exit, and walked down the mud grade to join Elara's intake tents an hour ago.

Farther north, Harken had made good on his threat. Wednesday at noon, Iron Creek and two neighboring high-crag mining camps had broadcast formal withdrawal notices across the spine, severing their regional treaties outright, freezing bilateral clearing accounts, and locking two tons of smelted vanadium-chromium alloy ingots and high-resin fuel briquettes behind snowed-in pass gates.

And out on the dry eastern flats, Blackwood Siding had crossed a line nobody had crossed since Year One. Refusing to reopen their outer water tap or hold a valid sanctuary ballot, Brenner's shop committee had been ruled in flat breach of the founding compact by a twelve-member sortition review panel drawn by lot across five watersheds. At dawn today, the network had revoked Blackwood Siding's trusted clearing keys altogether, dropping the rail-spur settlement outside the mutual-aid rings onto bare outsider terms.

"Forty-six hours, Elara," Elias said, coming up beside her with an empty oat-bread crate balanced on his hip, sleet crusted white in his beard. "Upper greenhouse loops dropped to forty-one degrees an hour ago because we're shunting every spare amp downhill to these tents. Two more nights of this draw and the safety relays trip automatically, or frost kills every winter kale bed on the terrace."

"Cut power to forty tents tonight in sleet and we carry bodies out at dawn, Elias," Elara said, rubbing snow out of her eyes with the back of her mitten.

"Wait forty-six hours and the automated cutout drops them anyway while leaving our own batteries flat," Elias answered plainly. "River barges sent dried peas this morning, and those nineteen walkout kids from the plateau brought medical bags and strong backs, bless 'em, but beans and bandages don't spin turbines. Without Thorne's main highland hydro ridges in the loop, one creek valley can't heat three thousand people."

At the base of Pole Four, Silas unbuckled his gaffs and slogged through the churned mud toward them, his left coat seam scorched black where a hot copper drop had caught the wool.

"Transformer Two won't survive another forty-eight hours at this amperage even if the battery floor holds," Silas said, blowing on his stiff knuckles once he pulled his rubber gauntlets off. "Oil inside the can's boiling."

Elara stared down at the regional diagnostic feed scrolling beneath the red floor banner on her slate. Half a minute went by while sleet ticked hard against the screen glass.

"Wait," Elara said slowly, zooming in on the public maintenance beacons still pinging every hour from Thorne's twelve main Northern Ridge fellowships. "Thorne didn't follow Harken into full withdrawal. She only froze surplus clearing. And look at East Peak's repeater status."

Silas leaned over her shoulder, squinting through wet hair.

"Wind Turbines Four, Seven, and Nine on East Peak have been sitting feathered on mechanical brakes since Wednesday night," Elara read off the telemetry flags. "Low gearbox oil pressure and two blown pitch-controller cards. Their mountain creeks are spilling bank-full November rain over the weirs with nowhere to dump excess nighttime kilowatts, while thirty percent of their wind fleet sits dead for lack of coastal marine lube and salvaged city circuit boards."

Silas looked sideways at her. "By freezing clearing channels, Thorne choked off Malik's oil drums and Priya's electronics runners."

"And Iron Creek walking out cut Thorne's high-ridge camps off from valley grain barges just when early blizzards closed their north pass," Elara said. "Get on the sideband radio to Thorne, Malik, and Priya. Tell them to meet us in the Foundry at six tonight."

"Thorne walked out at midnight Tuesday swearing she wouldn't hear another word about charity," Elias reminded her.

"Don't mention charity," Elara said, already turning toward the charging shed. "Tell her we have forty salvaged pitch-controller boards and five drums of coastal gear grease looking for twenty megawatts of spilled night hydro."

\bigskip

At six o'clock, Elara didn't take the delegates to a cold crossroads barn; she brought them straight onto the iron floor plates of the Foundry while Furnace One and all four biochar retorts were running hot.

Steam billowed off sleet-stiff coats ten feet inside the thermal curtains. The bay smelled sharply of hot cast iron, coal tar, charring alder, and wet wool, while the heavy thud of the drop-hammer at the far end of the pit shook water droplets off the steel columns.

Only nineteen regional speakers stood around the chalked floor pit tonight. Harken's Iron Creek bench was empty; Brenner's Blackwood Siding keys were dead at the outer gate; and the five Northern Plateau orchardists were home arguing with half-empty clinics after their own young stewards walked out.

Thorne stood with her wet sheepskin slung over one arm, pale eyes rimmed red from four days of hand-winching feathered turbine blades in freezing sleet. Malik stood across from her with fresh tar bandages on three fingers, while Priya leaned heavily against a mold cart drinking hot salted tea out of a tin cup, brick dust caked into her eyebrows.

"Everyone on a radio knows your battery floor trips Saturday morning, Elara," Thorne called over the furnace blowers without preamble. "If you dragged us through freezing rain to beg an emergency override on highland grain, save your breath. Harken already took three mining camps out of the compact entirely, and half my own elders wanted to follow him."

"Harken locked himself in a rock canyon with two tons of alloy steel and zero winter wheat or antibiotics," Elara answered evenly from the edge of the pit. "Let's see how warm metal keeps him in January. Meanwhile, Turbines Four, Seven, and Nine on East Peak have been sitting locked on dry gearboxes for thirty-eight hours, Thorne. How many more sleet storms before ice load snaps a stationary blade?"

Thorne's jaw tightened half a fraction; she didn't deny it.

"And Priya," Elara turned to the urban speaker, "your salvage crews have twenty crates of clean industrial pitch-controllers and breaker relays sitting in basement depots on Fourteenth Street, with zero charged battery trucks to haul them north because state troopers blockaded commercial diesel."

Priya set her tin cup down on the cart. "Thirty-two crates as of noon. Sitting in the dark."

"And Malik's coastal presses have twelve drums of heavy synthetic marine gear lube sitting on Dock Three while harbor runoff fouled their oyster flats and left two hundred dockhands short on flour and forged mooring cleats," Silas added beside the control stand.

Malik folded his arms. "All true. Still doesn't change the local floor rule, Elara. Nobody here has slack to write open checks to the whole basin."

"Then don't write a single open check," Elara said.

Silas threw a toggle on the crane pulpit. Overhead, the wide amber gantry display flickered alive, showing the eighty-odd remaining fellowships across the basin with red deficit markers and green surplus bars side by side. Three dark gray holes sat blank on the north and east edges where Iron Creek, Blackwood Siding, and the sanctioned plateau orchards had dropped out or been throttled.

"A majority vote forcing barns open would tear what's left of this Commonwealth in half tonight," Elara said, pointing up at the three dark gaps on the map. "We already lost Iron Creek's alloy mines and Blackwood's rail yard this week, and we're going to feel every missing pound of vanadium steel before winter's out. What keeps the rest of us standing isn't charity or central decrees. It's closing a trade ring where every link clears simultaneously in escrow."

She picked up a stick of soapstone and drew five heavy arrows connecting chalk circles right on the iron floor plates at their boots.

"Link one: Thorne opens the East Peak tie-line tonight and routes spilled nighttime creek hydro straight down to Malik's coastal desalination and smokehouse pumps," Elara said, rapping the chalk line with the stone. "Link two: Malik puts six drums of heavy marine gearbox grease on the upriver tug for Thorne's wind crews, plus four tons of smoked herring and dried kelp onto barges bound for our South Fork highway kitchens."

She drew the third and fourth lines across the iron. "Link three: coastal fish feeds the highway tents, taking forty percent of the calorie draw off our valley silos while highland hydro takes the heater load off our creek batteries. Link four: our Foundry pours forged dock cleats for Malik's piers and sends two fully charged electric flatbeds loaded with fifty bushels of hard red wheat down the back logging roads to Priya's urban kitchens. Link five: Priya's crews eat, load those same flatbeds with thirty crates of salvaged pitch-controller boards and copper busbars, and run them straight up the ridge road to Thorne's wind techs."

Elara straightened up, tossing the soapstone catch-hand. "Automated escrow switches verify every leg at the boundary. If one barge or kilowatt-hour fails to clear, no settlement's local floor gets drawn down by a single ounce. Nobody bleeds dry; everybody gets the missing part choking their own bay."

For ten seconds only the drop-hammer thumped down the hall.

Thorne stepped forward to the edge of the soapstone diagram, looking down at the five linked arrows and then up at the three dead gray holes on the overhead map where Harken's camps had vanished into winter silence. Engineers up on the ninety-day ridges knew what happened to isolated trusses when wind shifted.

"Escrow relays trip automatically before touching highland barley reserves?" Thorne asked quietly.

"Hardwired at your own substation breaker, Thorne," Silas said. "Your linemen hold the physical disconnect key."

Malik pulled his oil-stained slate from inside his pea coat. "Going to take some ugly arguing to settle swap ratios between kilowatt-hours, herring barrels, and forged dock iron."

"Kettle's hot on Retort Two," Elara said, wiping soot off her chin with her sleeve. "Pull up a crate."

For five grueling hours, mud-caked linemen, barge skippers, urban electricians, and millwrights crowded around sheet-iron worktables with soapstone and slates, haggling line by line over barge drafts, night-tariff kilowatt blocks, drum weights, and back-road truck schedules around state patrol checkpoints.

By eleven-thirty, thirty-four linked bilateral pacts were typed into the clearing queue:

\textit{Pact 08: Northern Ridge East Peak delivers 600 kWh nightly off-peak to Harbor Substation Three; Harbor Press Two ships 240 liters heavy gear lubricant upriver Tuesday.}

\textit{Pact 22: Valley Foundry casts 1,400 kg structural dock iron for Harbor Pier Seven; Harbor Smokehouse Four lands 1,800 kg cured herring at South Fork Landing.}

One after another, nineteen speakers stepped to the main terminal and pressed their signing fobs to the reader. Last in line, Thorne paused with her hand over the glass, glanced once more at the blank gray patch on the northern border where Iron Creek had cut itself loose, and pressed her key firmly down.

"Close the loop, Elara," Thorne said.

Elara keyed her counter-signature and threw the clearing latch.

Three seconds passed while substation nodes across seventy miles checked every local survival floor. Green clearances cascaded down the right-hand column, with eighty-two percent of the Commonwealth locking into load share and routing cleanly around the three severed holes on the map.

Outside in the sleet, the shrill, tortured whine of the pole transformers dropped a full octave into an even sixty-cycle hum as highland creek current surged down the ridge tie-line. On Elara's forearm slate, the forty-six-hour red floor warning winked out, climbing steadily back into safe amber.

Nobody cheered; people were too wrung out to waste breath. Delegates slumped onto wooden flask boxes with tin mugs of hot chicory, already comparing morning convoy times.

Silas walked over behind the terminal desk and leaned both forearms on the iron rail beside Elara.

"South Fork tent heaters are holding sixty-two degrees on highland hydro," he said quietly, checking his maintenance board. "Upper greenhouse pumps are back to full flow. Winter kale lives."

Elara let her shoulder rest briefly against his scorched sleeve, staring up at the gantry map. Bright green lines pulsed steadily from coast to ridge, yet up in the high crags and out on the eastern rail flats, Iron Creek and Blackwood Siding sat pitch-black and unreachable.

"Harken kept the autumn alloy train," Silas added in an undertone only she could hear over the blowers. "That hairline crack we found last week on Creek Turbine One's main runner: we won't have vanadium-chromium stock to recast it before deep freeze. Tobin and I are going to have to weld and cast patch blades out of salvaged truck-axle carbon steel tomorrow morning."

Elara looked at him sharply. "Will axle carbon hold if anchor ice hits the chute in January?"

Silas looked up at the dark northern gap on the screen, then down at his blackened knuckles. "Better than an empty scroll case," he said. "Just pray we get a mild January."
