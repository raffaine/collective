\chapter{Re-architecting the Space}

Under the tin roof of the abandoned freight depot in Sector Seven, the afternoon heat sat close to ninety-five degrees. Elara stood near the center chalk line with a canvas tool belt riding low on her hips, squinting through a haze of concrete dust and hot steel sparks. Around her, sledgehammers broke up frost-heaved floor slabs while two crews on rolling scaffolds cut rusted pallet racks down with angle grinders. For twenty years the tilt-up concrete shell had stood empty behind broken skylights and weeds; before the autumn rains arrived next week, it had to hold a tight roof over their new community hall, kitchen annex, and study rooms.

High above the floor, balanced on the top scaffold plank, Silas guided a cured black locust arch into its ceiling pocket. Harvested from the coppice woods two winters back, the round timber weighed close to four hundred pounds. His shirt was dark with sweat between his shoulder blades as he payed out the chain hoist link by link.

"Ease it down, Silas!" Elara shouted through cupped hands, her throat dry with lime grit. "You're an inch shy of the pocket!"

Silas kept his eyes on the joint. "I see it!" he called back over the shriek of a grinder. "The old steel roof girder has a twist in the flange. It's fighting the angle."

"Can we muscle it in?" Elara asked, stepping sideways to sight along the plumb bob.

"No," Silas grunted, pulling a heavy rubber dead-blow mallet from his belt loop. "Force it and the twist will split the printed collar. Let the wood find its own seat."

He swung the mallet in a short arc, striking the locust trunk squarely at its balance mark. With a low creak, the timber dropped half an inch and settled flush into the recycled-polymer socket bolted to the steel girder.

"Plumb on the south mark!" a rigger shouted from the opposite tower.

"Drive the bolts!" Silas called.

Three battery impact drivers clattered at once, running heavy galvanized lag bolts through the pre-drilled plates until the arch locked tight.

Elara wiped sweat out of her eyebrows with her wrist and checked the small slate strapped to her forearm. A single green check appeared beside Arch Seven. Tucked inside the collar, tiny strain gauges had just compared the settled load against the laser scan of the roof truss and logged the snow-load margin to the local server. Once it passed, the screen went dark again, leaving her eyes free for the floor.

Silas climbed down the scaffold ladder, his boots thudding onto the swept concrete. He pulled off his leather work gloves, tucked them under his belt, and shook wood shavings out of his hair.

"Last of the main ribs," he said, tipping his chin back toward the ceiling. Against the flaking gray oxide of the old steel girders, the seven curved locust arches gave the cavernous bay the look of a boat hull turned upside down.

"It'll outlast the concrete," Elara said.

Silas looked down at the gray dust caked around her eyes. His hand came up an inch off his hip, hesitated, and settled back onto the buckle of his tool belt.

"You've been running supply tallies and floor rigging since five this morning," he said quietly. "Go sit in the shade for twenty minutes."

"We're racing a cold front," Elara replied, glancing toward the open loading bay where heat shimmered off the cracked asphalt. "Six of the newest families are still sleeping in canvas wall tents by the orchard. Once the October rain sets in, canvas turns miserable fast. We need the study rooms framed and the annex stove piped before Friday."

"We'll beat the rain," Silas said. "Road crew called in on the ridge repeater five minutes ago. Bryn's bringing three electric vans through the gate right now with the pressed charcoal blocks and the wool sound-baffles from the looms."

Elara nodded, relief loosening the tight band across her neck. When the textile mill finished a batch and the kiln yard had blocks cured on pallets, the valley route board paired the loads with whatever charged vans were heading uphill, splitting battery draw and driver hours across the neighborhoods automatically.

"Let's snap the chalk lines for the interior partitions before she backs in," Elara said, hooking her tape measure off her belt. "Keep the quiet rooms along the east wall, away from the soup kettles."

For the next three hours, crews framed out curved stud walls of rough-sawn fir that broke up the echoing rectangle of the freight floor. Along the east side, carpenters hung heavy solid-core doors salvaged from an old municipal library on small reading nooks, packing the stud cavities with loose wool scrap so a person could shut the door and read for an hour without hearing a single saw or cookpot.

Along the bright south wall, beneath newly glazed skylights, the greenhouse crew bolted tiered cedar troughs to the concrete and fed rainwater lines down from the roof cisterns. Gabe knelt on an overturned bucket near the corner drain, his fingers black with wet peat as he showed one of David and Sarah's teenage boys how to seat brass compression fittings on the drip manifold. When Gabe cracked the main valve, clear water ticked steadily down through the fern roots and sheet moss. The new boy, who had spent his first two days in the valley staring at his shoes whenever an adult walked past, grinned and reached up to adjust a mister nozzle himself.

Two short blasts of an air horn echoed through the bay doors.

Three dented electric cargo vans rolled over the threshold in single file, their old delivery decals sanded down to bare aluminum and stenciled with the pine-and-river mark of the valley transport pool. Tires crunching over concrete grit, they backed cleanly up to the chalked unloading zone.

Bryn hopped down from the lead cab, dusted her palms on her overalls, and slapped the rear fender.

"Hot off the kilns and the felting tables!" she shouted across the floor. "Three tons of biochar-clay block and forty rolls of heavy wool baffle! Point me at a wall, Elara!"

"Charcoal blocks along the north concrete," Elara called back, waving toward the shaded side of the depot that caught the winter wind down the gorge. "Felt rolls right down the middle so the scaffold crew can hang them from the purlins."

Thirty people lined up from the open tailgates to the north wall. One block at a time, they passed the dense black bricks of pressed charcoal bound with slip-clay and chopped straw. Each block weighed fifteen pounds, smelled faintly of woodsmoke and river mud, and left black smudges across forearms and shirts. Silas worked the tailgate of the middle van, lifting blocks two at a time onto the staging plank, while Elara took the corner turn beside him, pivoting left and right until her triceps burned.

By five o'clock, the entire north wall stood packed floor-to-plate with black carbon brick and sheathed behind tongue-and-groove cedar planks. Halfway through the final course, Elara noticed the room had gone quiet. Even with people talking at the far end of the floor, the hard metallic slap of voices off bare concrete had vanished, swallowed by the porous charcoal walls and the thick gray wool clouds hanging between the roof beams.

Elara leaned back against the nearest locust arch and tipped her plastic water bottle upside down. Two warm drops hit her tongue. Charcoal dust coated her wrists and streaked her jaw where she had wiped sweat away.

Silas walked over and held out his dented aluminum canteen. Sweat had cut pale channels through the black dust in his beard.

"Drink," he said.

Elara unscrewed the cap, drank three long swallows of cold well water, and handed it back, her thumb brushing the grit on his fingers.

"Listen to the room," Silas said, looking down the length of the bay. Late sun slanted through the high south glass, lighting the wet green ferns on the living wall and throwing amber bars across the swept sawdust.

"Doesn't sound like a warehouse anymore," Elara said. "Tomorrow we can roll the trestle tables in and wire the reading lamps."

She looked up at him in the slanting light, catching the crow's-feet at the corners of his eyes and the gray streak at his temple that hadn't been there two summers ago.

"You're staring," Silas said, one corner of his mouth lifting.

"Checking if you're upright," Elara said, pushing an inch off the post.

Silas took a drink from the canteen and screwed the cap back on. "County assessor's truck parked by the cattle guard again this afternoon," he said quietly. "Sat there twenty minutes with binoculars, took pictures of the solar ridge, and turned around. And Meridian's regional office sent another certified letter about the substation tie-line."

"I saw the scan on the clerk board," Elara said. "We'll answer the letter Monday. Let them photograph the panels all they want."

Silas didn't answer right away. Keeping the careful half-step they always left between them in the workshops, he leaned back against the locust arch beside her so their sleeves touched.

For a minute neither of them spoke. Down at the far bay, Bryn slammed the last van doors shut, and Gabe laughed at something the new boy said while coiling up spare drip tubing.

Elara pushed off the timber and pulled her leather gloves from her belt. "Come on," she said. "Six more felt baffles on the west truss and we make first sitting at supper."
