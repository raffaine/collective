\chapter{The Elder Wisdom}

Four mornings after the snow-cats brought the Iron Creek survivors down out of the pass, Olander did not come down to breakfast.

At seventy-eight, his heart had simply stopped sometime between the two-o'clock stove check and dawn inside his small cedar room behind the pump shop. He had been one of the first twelve mechanics up this creek seven winters ago, hauling salvaged municipal water pumps up rutted mud tracks by hand-winch when everyone slept under leaking tarps.

By nine o'clock, pale January sun slanted through frost ferns on the reinforced glass of Olander's machine bay. Because workshop heat stayed shut off under the new sixty-percent hydro ration unless a critical repair was running, every breath hung white above the concrete floor. Olander's body, washed and wrapped in unbleached flax linen by Miriam and Lin, already lay resting in the cold stone cellar beneath the orchard shed awaiting burial once woodchip mulch thawed a trench.

All that remained on his oiled maple bench were the hand tools he had used for fifty years: drop-forged box wrenches worn smooth at the throat, brass plumb bobs, three Starrett outside micrometers nestled in green felt cases, and a ball-peen hammer whose hickory shaft carried the dark polish of his thumbprint.

Elara stood just inside the sliding door with her hands tucked deep in her coat sleeves, Silas beside her and sixteen-year-old Gabe half a step back looking pale around the mouth. Across the bench stood Miriam leaning on her ash walking stick, old Corin with frost-chapped ears sticking out beneath a knitted wool cap, and Tobin, whose bulky white hand-dressing rested inside a canvas sling pinned across his chest.

"Nobody calls a probate clerk today," Miriam said quietly, resting two gnarled fingers on the green felt lid of the micrometer box. "Out in the counties, a man dies and banks freeze his locks before his sister finishes crying, pricing out sixty years of craft by auction lot to settle hospital bills. In here, a tool only stays alive in a working hand."

Corin picked up Olander's heavy two-pound alignment maul and held it out handle-first toward Mara, who took it in both mittens to keep by the kiln grates. A roll of fine Swiss needle files went to Sam from Vera's electronics bay.

Then Miriam lifted the zero-to-one-inch steel micrometer out of its felt bed and looked past the older machinists straight at Tobin.

Tobin looked down at the bright satin-chrome spindle for two seconds. His right hand twitched once at his side, while the bandaged stump inside his sling stayed still.

"Give it to the boy, Miriam," Tobin said hoarsely. He looked across the cold bench at Gabe. "Takes two steady hands to roll a friction thimble while holding bar stock on center, kid. Olander told me in November you had good eyes on the inverter busbars, just rushed your layouts. Somebody with ten working fingers needs to read half-thousandths before spring plow shares wear thin, and I'll be checking your math from the tool crib."

Gabe swallowed hard, pulled off his right wool mitten, and took the cold steel frame carefully in both hands. Under his thumb, the tiny Vernier graduations shone crisp and clean without a speck of rust.

"Don't ever drop it on concrete, and wipe spindle oil on the threads before you shut the box," Tobin added gruffly, turning half away toward the window so nobody had to look at his face.

"Yes, sir," Gabe said softly. "I won't."

At sundown, rather than burning scarce cordwood and battery amps across forty separate cold cabins, two hundred people carried quilts, soup bowls, and carved stools into the insulated main hall of the Sector Seven depot to wake Olander under one shared roof.

Stepping through the wool baffle curtains out of twelve-below dusk felt like walking into another season. Two masonry stoves roared on dry coppice alder while body heat from two hundred neighbors raised the cedar-lined room past sixty-five degrees for the first time all week. Pots of salted barley-and-fish stew simmered on the top plates, smelling of smoked coastal herring, wild garlic, and roasted hubbard kraut.

High among the black locust roof arches hung forty-foot story-cloths woven on Riya's looms out of dyed fleece and salvaged glass optical strands that caught lantern glow in shifting threads of amber and blue: panels recording Jonas's pry-bar winter, the summer drone sweeps, and already a half-finished border of white river ice at the bottom edge. Near the hot updrafts above the char braziers spun suspended mobiles welded from cracked turbine scrap, drone props, and river slate, chiming softly in minor fifths whenever someone opened the vestibule door.

Nobody sat hushed in neat rows listening to speeches. Over by the living moss wall, six orchard hands and two of the young nurses who had walked down off the Northern Plateau were arguing cheerfully over a jug of dry pear cider, sketching new gravity-siphon pond layouts in window condensation with their fingertips. Near the soup kettles, eight ten-year-olds wearing grotesque cardboard hard-hats and spectacles cut from milk jugs staged a noisy slapstick play in which a pompous "County Subsoil Assessor" tried issuing a zoning ticket to a goat, sending even the bandaged Iron Creek miners propped on blankets along the south wall into rasping, reluctant coughs of laughter.

Around the central hearth pit, twelve drummers sat astride cured goat-hide barrels, tuned truck coil-springs, and gourds strung with steel radial-tire wire. Stripped down to his flannel shirtsleeves, his rib bandages hidden under dark wool, Silas picked up two leather-wrapped mallets and stepped to a halved propane tank tuned to a low, chest-rattling bass note. Three bars in, he locked squarely into the syncopated five-four pulse driving the circle.

Out on the swept sawdust floor, Gabe, with Olander's micrometer tucked safely back in his cabin chest, spun low into a fast, tumbling wheel game alongside Sam, Lia, and four river-barge teenagers, hands slapping pine planks as they vaulted over hooked sweeps and caught each other effortlessly by the wrists without breaking rhythm.

Standing near the locust arch with a tin mug warming her fingers, Elara watched Tobin tap his right boot heel steadily in time against a bench leg while pouring half a cup of pear cider one-handed for Harken's quiet sixteen-year-old nephew sitting beside him.

"Takes the chill out better than fifty-two-degree cabin pipes, doesn't it?"

Elara turned. Miriam leaned on her ash stick beside the arch, firelight glinting off the deep creases around her mouth. Back in the quieter reading nook behind them, Corin sat near a small iron stove paring thin curls off a willow peg.

"Half the valley walked in here tonight shivering and snapping at each other over wash-water turns," Elara said, raising her voice slightly over the tank drum. "Twenty minutes under the music and people remember they actually like living together."

"Come sit off your feet five minutes," Miriam said, nodding toward the bench beside Corin.

Inside the half-walled alcove the drumming softened to a warm wooden pulse through the floorboards. Elara sat down, letting out a slow breath as stove heat soaked into her sore knees from Tuesday's river dive.

"Don't mistake what's happening out on that floor for simple blowing off steam, Elara," Corin said without looking up from his pocketknife. "Back before the first creek trusts were signed, when Miriam and I were thirty years younger marching down Fourth Avenue behind union banners, we thought songs and street kitchens were side dishes to the 'real' politics."

Miriam rested both knotted hands atop her stick. "We lost three times in twenty years because we fought the old order strictly inside its own buildings. Filed fifty-page briefs before judges whose retirement funds owned the water board. Ran reform candidates who got starved out by bond markets inside six months. Called general strikes when nobody on the picket line knew how to bake bread without supermarket trucks or keep apartment boilers lit once the utility company pulled the master switch."

"Ten days without groceries and riot police barely needed batons," Corin said quietly, blowing a willow shaving off his thumb. "Hungry people went back to clocking in, or turned on each other over crumbs. Worse, nobody had a life outside buying cheap distractions on glass screens, so the moment management offered two dollars more an hour, half the line folded."

Miriam pointed her chin out past the locust arch where thirty voices had just joined a rough four-part work chorus over Silas's bass tank while children wove paper lanterns between the dancers' knees.

"Out there is why people wade into thirty-two-degree river water at five in the morning without a boss holding a gun or a paycheck over them," Miriam said. "Foundries and turnip cellars keep lungs breathing, Elara. Making weird, loud, unbought beauty together when half the power's out is what keeps a winter ration from turning neighbors into wolves."

Elara looked back through the arch. Out by the south wall, Harken hadn't joined the singing, still staring silently into his tin cup with his bandaged left foot propped on a crate, and down at the kiln sheds three men on late rotation had skipped the wake altogether, angry about pulling midnight stoking shifts in the cold. Even here, winter left rough splinters under the skin.

"State highway patrol parked two armored command vans at the Route Nine junction this afternoon," Elara said quietly to the two elders. "And nobody's heard a word out of Blackwood Siding since we pulled their keys, except diesel smoke rising by their rail spur."

"They'll test the fences hard before mud season," Corin agreed calmly. "Dying beasts always thrash worst right before the lungs quit."

Miriam laid her dry, warm palm briefly over Elara's wrist. "Tend the turbines, girl, and keep the legal clerks buried in boring paper as long as you can. Just don't ever let this valley get so grim about surviving winter that we turn into a barracks. Nobody holds a barracks wall to the end."

An hour before midnight, the fast drums slowed down to a single low heartbeat on wooden log-slits and bowed gourd strings. Silas handed his mallets off to a river boatman and walked through the crowd with sweat shining down his temples, sitting down beside Elara in the shadows of Arch Four and hooking one warm arm around her waist.

Tomorrow at nine they would carry Olander's linen-wrapped body up through two feet of crusted snow to the insulated compost vaults above the frost line, bedding him deep under warm woodchips, char, and hay so by May his bones would feed the young cherry roots along the upper terrace. Tonight, leaning her temple against Silas's damp flannel shoulder while two hundred voices hummed a slow wordless drone into the locust rafters, Elara let her eyes close for ten quiet minutes in the heat.
