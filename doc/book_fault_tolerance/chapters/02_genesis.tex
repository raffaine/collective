\chapter{Genesis}


The perimeter of the local node was not a wall. Walls were legacy architecture, brittle and authoritarian, demanding constant armed defense. The perimeter was a living boundary of crushed gravel, dense blackberry brambles, and small cameras tucked into the canopy of the oak trees. It breathed, it filtered, and today, it was choking on the exhaust of the old world. This sanctuary was merely one cell in the sprawling Polycentric Bioregional Commonwealth, a vast network of mutual aid that stretched across the fractured continent.

Elara stood at the edge of the access road, the afternoon sun beating down on her shoulders, heat shimmering off the cracked asphalt. Beside her, Marcus lowered a pair of dented field glasses.

"Five of them," Marcus muttered. "A family, maybe, or neighbors who stuck together. Looks like they walked the last ten miles from the state highway. Their car is dead on the shoulder."

Elara adjusted the canvas strap of her satchel, checking the onboarding kit against her hip. "Did the lookouts spot any county cruisers following them?"

"None," Marcus said, wiping his forehead with the back of his glove. "No drones, no patrol cars. Just them. Neighboring nodes sent word they might be heading our way."

As the group crested the rise of the access road, they were dragging their belongings in split duffel bags and a rattling utility wagon. The man in front was hollow-cheeked, his shoes bound at the soles with gray duct tape. Beside him, a woman clutched a sleeping toddler against her chest, her eyes darting along the tree line as if looking for pole cameras. Two teenagers trailed behind with their heads down.

Elara stepped forward on the gravel, carrying a clipboard and a steel thermos of cold well water.

"Stop there, please," Elara said.

The man flinched, dropping the handle of the wagon. He reached into his pocket with shaking fingers and pulled out a cracked smartphone and a plastic wallet. "We have the forms," he stammered, his voice dry. "I have my credit score, it's not great, but I can work. I have my background check from the county. We just need... we just need a place to stay. A lease. Anything."

"Put that away," Elara said, closing the distance between them. She unscrewed the cup of the thermos, filled it with water, and handed it to the woman holding the child. "We don't use credit scores here, and we don't care about county background checks. Drink first."

The woman took the cup in both hands and drank fast, water spilling down her dusty chin. The toddler stirred, letting out a thin whimper.

Elara glanced at the dark motor oil ground permanently into the creases of the man's knuckles. "What kind of work do you do?" she asked.

"Mechanic," he said, swallowing hard. "Small engines. Pumps. My wife, Sarah, runs industrial sewing machines."

Elara nodded once. Pumps and heavy canvas stitching were both backlogged three weeks.

"My name is Elara," she said, tapping the small screen on her wrist to log their arrival. "We don't do leases here, and we don't do debt. To come inside, though, you leave the county's tracking at this gate."

She pulled a weatherproof case from her bag and popped the latches on an offline circuit board and two blank keycards. The man stared at the electronics and took half a step back.

"What is that?" he asked. "Is that a scanner? I told you, I have my ID—"

"It's a local key maker," Elara interrupted evenly. "It doesn't connect to the internet or report to any agency. It stays on our wires. I can't hand you workshop access automatically, though. Somebody inside has to vouch for you in person."

Watching Sarah pass the tin cup to her older boy, Elara smelled the hot road dust and remembered standing in November mud seven years earlier, when the settlement was fifty frightened people and a leaking warehouse roof.

\bigskip

\textit{They had called that first winter the System Call, and their own software had nearly killed them.}

\textit{Jax had designed an automated ledger to track every chore, kilowatt, and blanket ration, convinced that clean math would keep fifty desperate strangers fair. Then an early freeze hit. A bug in the heating priority script routed night power to cool the server racks instead of warming the nursery.}

\textit{At three in the morning, with frost forming on the cribs, a carpenter named Jonas took a steel pry-bar to the main server cabinet, screaming that the machine was freezing his daughter. Elara still remembered kneeling in icy mud outside the kitchen with numb fingers, splicing torn cable by flashlight while people shouted in the rain.}

\textit{Around a burn barrel before dawn, Kael had thrown Jax's automated ration scripts out altogether. From that morning on, no card unlocked a heavy tool or drew workshop power unless a living neighbor stood beside you, looked you in the eye, and staked their own workshop standing on yours. Kael had gone first, ladling out the bottom of the pot of rice and beans, handing Elara a tin bowl in the rain, and signing his name beside hers.}

\bigskip

The snap of the plastic latch brought Elara back to the sunny road.

The mechanic, who had given his name as David, was watching her hands.

"We need your phones," Elara said, holding out a shielded Faraday pouch. "Phones, watches, anything cellular. The county uses tower pings to estimate headcount out here."

David hesitated, thumb resting on the cracked glass of his phone, then dropped it into the bag. Sarah added hers, and the two teenagers handed theirs over without a word.

"Marcus?" Elara said, sealing the pouch and tossing it into the utility cart.

Marcus stepped up to the wagon with a handheld inventory reader, tagging the oil-stained steel toolbox and the canvas-wrapped machine behind it.

"Metric socket and wrench set for the repair bay," Marcus read off. "Industrial serger for the textile loft."

"Those tools go into the shop pool, though you have first use of them," Elara told David. "We have a four-bed timber cabin open near the west orchard. Heater, running water, lock on the door."

"On what terms?" David asked, wiping his mouth. "A loan? What's the interest rate? Because if it's like the payday places—"

"No interest and no rent," Elara said. "You get power, groceries, and a roof. Once you've slept, you help keep the pumps and sewing machines running. When our valley grows extra, it goes down the road to neighboring settlements; when we're short, they send us what we lack."

They loaded the bags and the wagon onto the electric cart and drove slowly up the shaded gravel lane past the willow coppice and solar-roofed cabins, rolling to a stop outside the open timber doors of the Commons Kitchen. Inside, the evening crew was chopping onions and scraping carrots at long prep tables while soup kettles steamed on the electric ranges.

"Food and a roof are unconditional here," Elara said, stopping beside the heavy oak table in the center of the room, where a small brass-rimmed card reader sat flush in the wood. "Even if you never lifted a wrench, nobody here gets evicted or goes hungry. Running high-voltage lathes and shop equipment is different. That takes a co-signer."

"A background check," David said, his shoulders dropping. "Look, my credit is shot. I had a dispute with a landlord, they put an eviction on my record—"

"Nobody here cares about your landlord," Elara said, setting two blank wood-veneered cards on the oak table. "To unlock the machine bays, a steward here has to tie their own workshop key to yours."

"Who's going to do that?" David asked, glancing at the cooks watching quietly from the prep counters. "Nobody knows us."

"I will," Elara said.

Marcus set down the crate of tomatoes he was carrying. "Elara, hold on. You're carrying the administrative keys for the whole hub. If he wrecks a lathe or hurts someone, your own clearance freezes during review."

"I know the rule, Marcus," Elara said. She looked straight at David and Sarah. "If you strip parts from the shop or hurt a neighbor, my access gets suspended right alongside yours. That's the deal."

David looked down at the wooden card on the table, then back up at her. "Why?"

"Because somebody signed for me when I walked in off the highway," Elara said. She tapped the brass rim of the table reader to wake it. "Put your cards beside my ring."

David and Sarah stepped up and pressed their cards against the oak next to Elara's hand. The indicator blinked blue once and went dark, logging the co-signature locally.

Elara reached for a stack of stoneware bowls, ladled three full from the nearest stockpot, and pushed a basket of sliced bread across the wood.

"Paperwork's done," Elara said, setting a spoon beside David's bowl. "Sit down and feed the little one before the soup cools."
