\chapter{The Brink of Severance}


The air tasted of copper and burning plastic. It was a taste Elara hadn't known this sharply since the first days they broke ground in the valley, a harsh reminder that the perimeter they had so carefully tended was only a boundary line, not an iron wall. The wind, which usually carried the scent of lavender from the clinic garden or damp earth from the hillside terraces, was blowing hard from the east today. From the direction of the city. From the dying heart of the old world.

Elara stood on the high catwalk of the main solar array, gripping the galvanized steel railing. Her hands were calloused, ingrained with foundry grease and the dark loam of the terraces. Below her, the valley stretched out in a living patchwork of timber roofs, stone retaining walls, and green orchards, standing in stark contrast to the bruised horizon. 

Fifty miles east, the skyline of the city stood jagged and dark against a dirty, yellow-gray sky. It wasn't the slow, quiet twilight of an empire that the old books had imagined. It was a violent, chaotic unraveling. The beast was dying, and it knew it. Columns of thick, oily black smoke rose into the sky, unchecked by municipal fire departments that had collapsed months ago when the city defaulted on its pensions and ran dry of diesel. At night, the eastern horizon glowed a restless, angry orange with unchecked electrical fires and blown transformers, a society burning its own floorboards to stay warm for one more hour.

Across the county line, cities and towns had fractured into desperate, predatory fiefdoms. Bankrupt municipalities had defaulted on their bonds and abandoned whole neighborhoods to the dark. In response, the ultra-wealthy had retreated into fortified suburban compounds, walled enclaves ringed with razor wire, automated gun towers, and private security contractors. Inside those gated fortresses, hydroponic bays and diesel generators hummed behind locked gates for the few who could still pay, while the miles of sprawl around them sank into misery.

In the hollowed-out spaces between the compounds and the downtown core, across the dead strip malls, rusted rail yards, and half-empty subdivisions, armed scavenging crews and extortion gangs ruled the streets. They preyed on whoever was left behind, siphoning fuel, stripping copper pipe from abandoned houses, and acting as hired muscle for corrupt precinct bosses who traded food rations for loyalty.

And pressed right against the edge of that spreading rot was the valley. 

Elara’s settlement, nestled in the fertile basin beyond the outer suburbs, was catching the full force of the human wave. For weeks, the perimeter gates had been crowded with exhausted families who had walked thirty miles through dangerous highways with babies tied to their chests, carrying whatever heirloom seeds, hand tools, or winter coats they could carry on their backs. The community took them in, washing the toxic soot from their clothes, wrapping them in wool blankets, and finding room for them at the kitchen tables and timber lodges. Still, the sheer number of hungry, frightened people arriving every day was stretching their stores and their soil to the limit.

Worse still were the escalating threats from the state authorities. The regional power monopoly, Meridian Grid Solutions, had sent armed marshals to the valley gate three times this month, demanding access to the community’s river turbines and solar arrays under an "emergency eminent domain seizure." The state attorney general had filed a flood of court injunctions, calling the valley’s independent power grid an "unlawful threat to regional stability" and accusing the stewards of harboring runaway debtors. 

They weren't just trying to tax the community anymore; they were trying to strip it for parts.

``Wind’s shifting,'' a deep voice rumbled behind her.

Elara didn't need to turn around. She knew the heavy, steady cadence of Silas's boots on the iron grating. He stepped up beside her, resting his thick forearms on the rail. He smelled of hot iron, flux, and grinding sparks from the foundry. His beard was dusted with fine steel filings, and his dark eyes, usually so calm, were fixed on the eastern horizon with a hard, watchful intensity.

``It’s speeding up,'' Elara said quietly, her voice barely carrying over the wind. ``The city councils in the eastern districts just passed an emergency seizure order. Any independent power generation within fifty miles of the city line is subject to immediate confiscation by state energy marshals. They're starving for electricity, Silas. Their natural gas turbines are seizing up for lack of parts, and they look at our valley like an unlocked fuel tank.''

Silas nodded slowly, his knuckles whitening on the rail. ``The ridge lookouts have been calling in movement all morning. Not just county marshals, either. Armed pickup convoys from the old highway belt were testing the northern fence line before sunrise. They tried to hook a tow cable to the pasture gate near the sheep pens. Gabe and the dawn watch drove them off with flare pistols and sound horns, but they'll be back with more trucks. When a wolf gets hungry enough, it stops caring about the fire.''

``A dying beast doesn't just starve,'' Elara murmured, remembering the cold dread of her own departure from the city ten years ago. ``It lashes out at anything it can reach. Everything we've built here, our shared kitchens, our circles of accountability, and our terraced fields, depends on having enough breathing room to heal what the city broke. What happens when the whole dam gives way at once?''

``We hold our ground,'' Silas answered, turning to face her. His rough, grease-stained hand covered hers on the steel rail, the steady warmth of his palm anchoring her against the cold wind. ``We didn't build a glass conservatory, Elara. We built strong walls with a living garden inside. We always knew this day would come. That machine out there was never going to lie down quietly. It was always going to try to pull us into the fire with it.''

Before Elara could reply, the brass horn at the foot of the tower sounded with two short blasts followed by a low, steady hum. It was the call to the communications room.

``Priya is on the line,'' Silas said, glancing down at the worn slate on his wrist. ``The city crews just opened their scheduled relay window.''

They hurried down the steel stairs and crossed the busy heart of the settlement. All around them, neighbors were preparing for rough weather of a human kind. Crews were bolting salvaged polycarbonate storm panels over the greenhouse roofs. Down by the foundry, welders were joining heavy scrap I-beams into interlocking road barriers. Yet in the middle of it all, children were still laughing in the schoolhouse yard, playing a game with painted river stones under the steady eye of Corin. Life didn't stop for the gathering storm; it simply sank its roots deeper.

Down in the vaulted cellar beneath the old stone textile mill, the air was cool and smelled of damp fieldstone and warm copper wiring. Rows of salvaged monitors and e-ink screens glowed softly with maps of the surrounding valleys and ridge relays.

Maya stood before the main table, the pale green light of the screen reflected in her eyes, while Gabe stood close beside her, one hand resting on her shoulder. On the center monitor, a grainy video feed resolved into the sharp, sleepless face of Priya.

Priya was calling from deep inside the city. Behind her was no office or meeting hall, only the ribbed concrete arch of an abandoned subway service tunnel lit by a single string of battery lanterns. Her dark hair was tied back with a strip of canvas, her cheek smudged with soot, yet her eyes burned bright and clear.

``Elara, Silas, good to see you both,'' Priya’s voice crackled over the tight beam relay bounced from the ridge towers. ``We’ve got twenty minutes before city signal-vans triangulate our rooftop mirror and we have to break down the link.''

``How bad is it in there, Priya?'' Elara asked, stepping up beside Maya. ``We can see the smoke from the solar tower. Did the police hit the Fourteenth Street depot?''

``They hit it hard,'' Priya said with a dry, humorless laugh. ``Three days ago, the mayor signed an emergency order declaring every independent neighborhood kitchen in the fourth district an unlicensed health hazard. City tactical squads backed by private security trucks battered down the doors on Fourteenth Street. They smashed the fish tanks, hauled off our solar batteries, and put twelve of our young volunteers in handcuffs.''

Gabe drew a sharp breath, his fists clenching at his sides. ``Did they shut you down?''

``They smashed an old brick warehouse, Gabe. They didn't touch us,'' Priya said fiercely. ``Because two weeks ago, the city crews voted to change how we work. We've gone completely mobile.''

Silas leaned forward over the table, his brow furrowed. ``Mobile? How do you feed thousands of people without a fixed kitchen?''

``By never giving them a target twice,'' Priya said, her fingers tapping a rugged slate off-camera and sending a fresh set of city map markers across Maya's screen. ``Holding onto permanent buildings in a city under martial law is a trap. It just gives the police a front door to batter down and gives the street syndicates an address to shake down. So we packed the whole network onto wheels.''

She tilted her camera slightly to show the tunnel behind her. Dozens of young volunteers were breaking down a high-efficiency field kitchen and strapping water filters and medical packs onto heavy cargo trikes and light electric vans.

``We move every forty-eight hours,'' Priya explained. ``We set up soup kettles, water filters, and first-aid cots in old subway passages, dead rail sidings, or church basements. We feed and treat the neighborhood for twelve hours straight, and then we vanish before the raid vans can organize. We load everything onto cargo bikes, bounce our messages off upper-story office windows with signal lamps, and set up three miles away in another ward by nightfall.''

``That pace must be exhausting,'' Elara said quietly, her heart aching at the hollow dark circles beneath Priya's eyes.

``It’s hard on the bones,'' Priya admitted, her shoulders sagging for just a breath. ``Nobody's getting more than four hours of sleep on cold concrete, and we're living on dried beans and trail cakes. But it's working. The tactical squads are swinging at air. They spend half a day ramming the doors of an empty basement, burn through their last drums of diesel, and find nothing inside but a chalk arrow on the wall pointing neighbors to our next clinic. Better yet, whole apartment blocks are standing watch for us now. When the city shut off the public hydrants and our bike crews showed up two hours later with river filters, people saw who actually cared whether their kids drank clean water. We aren't just dodging the collapse anymore, Elara. The neighborhoods are coming over to us.''

A sharp beep sounded from Priya’s slate, and a red light blinked at the corner of her camera.

``Scanner vans are two blocks away,'' Priya said briskly, her voice dropping low. ``We’re pulling the plug. Listen closely, though: the state government is panicking because they're losing the factory towns and the farm belt too. Silas, check the shortwave reports from the western rail lines. It isn't just the city that's coming apart.''

The screen went dark with a soft pop of static.

Silas reached across the table and brought up the regional shortwave dispatches that the radio crew logged from neighboring valleys and rail towns. Across the map, red markers clustered along the old industrial river corridors.

``She’s not exaggerating,'' Silas said, his voice low and grave. ``Look at the factory belt along the river.''

``Supply lines finally snapped,'' Silas continued, tracing a scarred finger along a chain of red markers on the rail map. ``When the regional grid began failing last month, the industrial combines tried to lock their machinists inside the plants under armed guard, running backup diesel generators day and night to keep stamping out Riot-Control drones and patrol vehicles. Last week, four major engine plants walked out on wildcat strikes and occupied the shop floors. The companies sent hired security contractors in to clear the buildings by force. Before the workers were driven out, they dumped water straight into the white-hot slag pits and cracked the main foundry crucibles in half. Heavy manufacturing across the whole tri-state corridor is dead in the water.''

``What about the valley farms to the south?'' Elara asked, a cold weight settling in her stomach.

``Even uglier,'' Silas said grimly. ``The big agribusiness combines have turned their estates into armed camps. They’ve strung electrified wire around ten-thousand-acre corn and soy fields, with patrol drones flying the fence lines. Inside, they've got desperate migrant families working for food scrips behind locked gates. Outside the wire, the small country towns are starving on dead topsoil and poisoned well water. Any independent farmer who refused to sell out is having their irrigation ditches blocked and their tractors seized by private bailiffs over trumped-up bank liens.''

``Feudalism with drones,'' Elara murmured, shaking her head. ``Turning bread into a leash.''

``Yes,'' Maya spoke up from the table, her voice steady and clear against the grim news. ``Except they are still counting on one thing that isn't true anymore. They think the state bureaucracy, the courts, the clerks, the utilities, and the highway patrol, still belongs entirely to them.''

Elara looked over at Maya. ``What do you mean?''

A quiet, knowing smile touched Maya’s lips as she typed a passkey into her slate. A second map overlaid the regional chart in warm amber lines, showing not a map of roads or rivers, but of county offices, water boards, and court dockets.

``We haven't just been digging ditches and bolting solar panels for the last five years, Elara,'' Maya said, gesturing to the web of amber lines. ``While the state capital wrote us off as a harmless band of back-to-the-land dreamers, Kael’s early legal networks were quietly building bridges inside every county office in this region.''

Silas leaned in, his eyes widening. ``The civil servants.''

``Every one of them,'' Maya nodded. ``State agencies aren't made of iron; they're staffed by ordinary people whose kids need clean water, decent food, and a world that isn't on fire. All across this county and the three beside it, people who share our table sit behind county desks. They're water-board engineers, county clerks, emergency dispatchers, road-crew foremen, and even sheriff’s deputies and state troopers who put on a uniform to protect their neighbors, not to act as debt collectors for holding companies.''

Maya tapped a file from the regional water authority.

``Look here,'' she said, pointing to a stamped directive on the screen. ``Eight days ago, the governor’s office ordered the regional water board to shut the headgates feeding our valley canal. Do you know why the water in our ditches never dropped an inch? Because the chief hydrologist for the district has been trading heirloom grain with our mill for three years. He reclassified our valley intake as an 'essential wetland conservation spillway,' burying the governor's shutoff order in an environmental review docket that takes eighteen months to clear. And when the state police requisitioned flatbed transports to haul concrete barricades up to our ridge road, a foreman at the highway depot red-tagged the whole truck fleet for contaminated fuel lines.''

``From the inside out,'' Elara breathed, staring at the amber map in wonder.

``It isn't sabotage; it's people choosing their neighbors over a dying machine,'' Maya said gently. ``Those clerks and engineers are using the bureaucracy's own red tape to tie the state's hands behind its back. They misfile predatory foreclosure warrants. They sign over impounded farm equipment to rural tool libraries. They warn us days in advance before an inspection team leaves the capital. The governor is trying to swing an iron sledgehammer at this valley, but the handle is made of ordinary working people, and it's giving way in his hands.''

``Is this happening beyond our state?'' Silas asked.

``All over the map,'' Maya said, zooming out to the continental and overseas shortwave logs. ``Along the Mediterranean coast, water cooperatives have walked away from the central banks altogether, trading power and grain directly between valleys and port towns. High in the Andes, indigenous commonwealths have linked our open-source ledgers with their ancestral land councils to shut down foreign mining syndicates. In the military dictatorships and oil states, the crackdowns have been bloody, with settlements shelled and organizers thrown into prisons, yet every time they crush one hub, three more light up downriver with the same shared blueprints. You can bomb a workshop, but you can't un-teach a million people how to feed and govern themselves.''

Suddenly, the cellar’s warm lantern light switched to a pulsing red warning beacon.

A deep, resonant chime rang through the stone room, an alert tone Elara had heard tested only once in her life. 

Maya’s breath caught, her fingers flying across the keys as lines of verification code scrolled down the center monitor.

``What is that?'' Gabe asked, gripping the edge of the table. ``Did the state signal vans find our bunker?''

``No,'' Maya whispered, her eyes wide with astonishment. ``That’s not an attack. It’s an incoming beacon from our own laser receiver on the western ridge. And it’s signed with an original founder’s key.''

Elara felt her heart skip a beat. Only two people on earth carried those keys. 

The center monitor cleared, replacing the regional maps with four stark lines of text:

\begin{center}
\textbf{WEST RIDGE GATE: THREE-TRUCK CONVOY APPROACHING.}\\
\textbf{KEY VERIFIED: KAEL \& JAX.}\\
\textbf{ROUTE: WESTERN CONTINENTAL HIGHWAY.}\\
\textbf{REQUESTING IMMEDIATE ENTRY.}
\end{center}

``From the west,'' Silas rumbled, the words heavy as iron. ``Three thousand miles across the interior highways.''

``Kael and Jax,'' Elara said, her voice trembling as a wave of fierce, overwhelming relief rose in her chest. ``They're alive. They actually made it back.''

``If those two are pulling back to our valley,'' Silas said, his jaw setting hard, ``then the interior highways are finished. Come on, we need to be at the western gate before the rams open.''

They ran up the stone stairs and burst out into the bright afternoon sun. Across the valley, the deep chime of the gate horn was still echoing off the hills. 

Around them, the settlement was already moving with practiced calm. Out in the pastures, herders were guiding the sheep into the stone-walled lower barns. Down by the workshops, welding torches were shut off and road crews were rolling heavy steel tire-spikes into position beside the gatehouse. At the schoolhouse, Corin and the teachers were leading the children down into the vaulted stone cellars beneath the mill. Nobody panicked or shouted; everyone knew their place.

By the time Elara, Silas, Maya, and Gabe reached the western gate, nearly two hundred neighbors stood waiting in a quiet, watchful half-circle. 

The western gate looked less like a fort and more like part of the hillside itself, built of stacked steel shipping containers filled with river rock and rammed earth, and overgrown with thick tangles of perennial hops, wild grapevine, and thorny blackberry brambles. With a deep hydraulic groan that vibrated through the soles of their boots, the heavy steel doors swung slowly outward.

Clouds of pale road dust rolled in off the cracked two-lane highway stretching west toward the dry interior plains. 

Then, rumbling out of the dust cloud like iron beasts emerging from a furnace, came three trucks.

The two trailing rigs were heavy diesel flatbeds loaded with round biodiesel tanks, tarped machine lathes, and crates of salvaged battery cells. The lead rig, however, looked like a rolling workshop built to cross the end of the world.

It was an eight-wheeled articulated heavy hauler, its cab plated in welded sheets of rusted Corten steel. Bolted to its frame was a massive, angled steel plow built to shove burned-out cars and concrete highway barriers out of its path. Its roof carried a thicket of folding solar panels, long-range radio whips, and directional signal dishes, while its eight tall tires were caked in red clay, white alkali salt, and gray ash from a dozen states. 

The heavy hauler rolled to a stop fifty feet inside the gate. Its air brakes let go with a loud hiss that kicked up little eddies of dust around the crowd's boots. For a moment the big engine idled with a deep, steady thrum, burning thick, sweet-smelling vegetable oil before shutting down into silence.

For ten long seconds, nobody moved. The only sound was the wind whistling through the brambles on the gate and the sharp ticking of cooling exhaust pipes.

Then the heavy steel driver’s door swung open on dry hinges.

Kael climbed down from the cab.

The man who had helped spark the movement across a continent didn't look like a legend out of a story. He looked like a man carved from weathered oak, leather, and iron. Tall and spare, leaned down by years on the road until only sinew and bone remained, he carried the miles in every line of his face. Deep creases fanned out around his gray eyes from squinting through dust storms and glaring sun; his thick beard was heavily streaked with silver, and his canvas coat was patched at the elbows and knees with heavy leather and braided copper wire.

He moved like a man carrying an immense weight on his shoulders, bent not by weakness, but by everything he had witnessed along the road.

Elara took a half-step forward, her breath catching in her throat. Ten years ago, Kael had been the senior architect who found her sitting in a grey downtown office tower, slowly drowning in the quiet despair of corporate finance. He was the one who had shown her the way out, handing her a key to the early network and reminding her that a human life was meant for more than servicing debt. Seeing him standing here in the dust of their valley, older and scarred but alive, broke through her composure. Her eyes stung with sudden tears.

Before she could speak, the passenger door swung wide.

Jax dropped lightly from the high running board, landing on the gravel with the coiled alertness of a bobcat. 

Where Kael was the quiet, patient architect, Jax had always been the sharp edge of the blade. Younger and wire-thin, he had shaved the sides of his head close, his dark braided locks tied back with a bit of insulated copper wire. Over a faded mechanic’s coverall he wore a canvas tool vest packed with spare radio batteries, fiber-splicing tools, signal meters, and a worn service pistol holstered snug against his ribs. Before his boots had even settled in the gravel, his dark eyes had swept the entire gate, checking the sightlines on the ridge, the thickness of the earthworks, and the faces of the crowd.

Neither of them looked like utopian dreamers. They looked like two road-worn scouts returning from the front lines of a storm that was fast overtaking the horizon.

Elara stepped forward from the half-circle, Silas right beside her like a wall of granite.

``Kael,'' Elara said, her voice catching yet carrying clearly across the silent yard. ``Jax. You made it home.''

Kael stopped. His gray eyes found Elara. For a heartbeat, the guarded mask of the highway dropped away. He looked at her, taking in the kiln soot on her brow, the calluses on her palms, and the steady, quiet strength in the way she stood, before looking past her shoulder at the green terraced hillsides, the glinting solar roofs, the clear water running through the stone ditches, and the two hundred healthy, unbroken people standing at her back.

A slow, unsteady breath left Kael’s chest. His eyes shone bright in the sun.

``Elara,'' Kael said, his voice rough as gravel. ``Look at what you’ve grown here. Look at what you kept alive.''

He closed the distance between them and laid a scarred, calloused hand on her shoulder, not as a teacher checking on a student, but equal to equal, an old builder greeting the one who had held the roof up through the storm.

``We barely cleared the plains, Elara,'' Kael said quietly, his voice heavy with the miles behind them. ``The middle of the country... it’s hollowed out. The federal ministries in the interior have completely fractured.''

Jax stepped up beside him, his expression taut. Reaching into his canvas vest, he pulled out a heavy, lead-lined hard drive case and set it down hard on the steel fender of the truck.

``We don't have time for a reunion dinner, Elara,'' Jax said sharply, his voice cutting straight to the bone. ``We didn't drive three thousand miles through burning roadblocks and militia checkpoints just to admire the orchards. We're out of time.''

Silas took a step closer, his broad chest squaring as he met Jax’s gaze. ``Tell us what's coming down the highway, Jax. Plain and simple.''

Jax looked up at the big machinist, gave a tight nod of respect, and tapped the metal drive case. 

``The dying beast is making its last lunge,'' Jax said. ``For the last eight months, Kael and I have been tracing the dispatches moving between the state capitals and the remaining federal command bunkers. What you're seeing in the city, the bankruptcies, the gangs, and the rich locking themselves inside gated compounds, is just the surface rot. Behind closed doors, the governors and the bank cartels have finally realized they can't starve you back into their factories. They see whole counties walking away from their currency. They see their tax base and their workforce disappearing into the valleys.''

Kael turned to face the gathered stewards, his weathered voice rising so everyone at the gate could hear.

``They are calling it the Emergency Reclamation Act,'' Kael said. ``The state governors and the utility cartels have decided that if they cannot force the autonomous valleys back onto the grid, they will crush them before the rest of the public follows your example. They aren't sending county marshals with clipboards anymore. Right now, state riot battalions and contracted security convoys are staging along the interstate to move on every major settlement in this basin.''

A low murmur ran through the crowd. Beside Maya, Gabe’s hand tightened on the shaft of his pitchfork as he thought of little Clara sleeping down in the stone cellar.

``They intend to seal the valley roads first,'' Kael continued, his voice steady and unflinching. ``They plan to cut the remaining trunk cables, spray defoliants across the upper hillside crops, and roll armored bulldozers through the gates under the banner of restoring state order. They mean to make an example of this valley so no other town dares to cut the cord.''

Elara felt a cold knot tighten in her stomach, yet beneath the fear, something far steadier took hold: the same stubborn resolve that had carried them through the winter river, the drought, and the long nights in the foundry.

``They can't hold a siege like that without legal cover,'' Elara said, her mind racing clearly through the pieces on the board. ``Even now, their own agencies are hanging by a thread. If the governor orders troopers and National Guardsmen to bulldoze farming families without a lawful warrant, half their own ranks will mutiny, especially with Maya's network of civil servants inside the county offices.''

``Which is why they’re rushing the warrant through the capital right now,'' Jax answered grimly. ``In forty-eight hours, the state legislature and the emergency appellate court are convening behind closed doors. They’re going to declare the entire Commonwealth an unlawful secessionist cartel. With one stroke of a pen, they plan to void every land trust, cooperative charter, and agricultural easement you've used to shield this valley since the beginning. Once the court strips your legal standing, they give the order to march.''

Kael looked straight at Elara, his gray eyes steady and intense.

``The quiet years are over, Elara,'' Kael said softly. ``The hope that we could keep growing in the cracks of the old world without them forcing a final showdown is gone. There is only one card left to play, the one we drafted ten years ago and hoped we wouldn't need for another generation.''

Elara closed her eyes for a heartbeat. The wind blew across her face, carrying the smoky bite of the eastern horizon mixed with the clean scent of pine and river water from their own hills.

She knew what he meant. Every steward who had studied the founding charters knew the name of that final step.

``Full decoupling,'' Elara said quietly, the words settling like bedrock.

``Full decoupling,'' Kael nodded. ``We don't wait for their kangaroo court to void our corporate charters; we dissolve those shells ourselves before they ever gavel in. And we replace them in open court with something older than their state altogether: the sovereign treaty pact we've spent the last two years forging with Tahoma and the Indigenous nations whose ancestral land rights predate the state constitution itself. We sever every remaining legal, financial, and utility tie to the municipal grid at once.''

``If we cut those last ties,'' Silas said, his deep voice quiet and grave, ``there's no turning back. No shell companies to hide behind, no stalling them with paperwork. When their riot lines reach the gate, it'll just be our people, our land, and our nerve against their shields and batons.''

``It has always been our people and our land, Silas,'' Elara said, opening her eyes. All the lingering hesitation of the past months fell away, leaving her mind clear and sharp. ``Those paper charters were only scaffolding while the mortar dried. A court in the capital didn't give us the right to feed our children. This soil did. This river did. Our own hands did.''

She turned to face her neighbors gathered inside the gate: the farmers, the machinists, the midwives, and the young apprentices. She saw Gabe standing tall beside Maya, his jaw set with the quiet resolve of a new father. She saw Miriam and Corin leaning on their staffs at the front of the crowd. She saw Silas standing solid as oak at her side.

The storm had reached the ridge. The dying machine was coming for them. And nobody in the valley was backing down.

``Get word to the radio room,'' Elara called out, her voice ringing clear and steady across the gate yard. ``Call an emergency assembly across every settlement and hub in the Commonwealth. Send a runner to Priya’s mobile crews in the city, and let Tahoma and the tribal council know that Kael is here with the filings. Fire up the foundry and double the watch on the ridge roads. We have forty-eight hours to stand this valley on its own feet for good.''

She turned back to Kael and Jax, a fierce, steady smile touching her lips.

``Welcome home, you two,'' Elara said. ``Let's go cut the last cord.''
