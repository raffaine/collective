\chapter{Maintenance and Entropy}

Two weeks into January, the temperature dropped to nine below zero and stayed there.

Elara woke before dawn in Silas's mill loft to the quiet tick of cooling copper under the floorboards and the weight of Silas's arm across her waist. Outside the mycelium-insulated rafters, a dry arctic gale hammered sleet and ice pellets sideways against the tin siding. Even under two heavy wool blankets, cold drafts crept around the window sash and bit the tip of her nose.

Careful not to bump the healing burn scar along Silas's left ribs, Elara eased out from under the covers and pulled on thick wool long-johns, greased canvas trousers, and two sweaters in the dark. Ever since Silas and Tobin had bolted the patched runner back into Creek Turbine One after New Year's, using replacement blades cast from salvaged highway truck-axle steel because Harken's Iron Creek mines had kept the autumn vanadium-chromium ingots locked behind their pass, Elara had woken up listening automatically for the faint sixty-cycle hum through the wall studs.

As she bent to pull her bootlaces tight, the yard bell let go.

Hung from the steel tower outside the machine shop, the salvaged locomotive bronze boomed fast and flat under its trip-hammer, vibrating straight up through the pine soles of her socks. Overhead, the loft bulb flickered twice, dropped to dim orange emergency voltage, and stayed there as the battery house picked up the dead bus.

Silas rolled off the mattress in one motion, reaching for his insulated bibs before his boots even hit the planks.

"Gorge feeder dropped flat," he said, voice thick with sleep as he shoved his arms into his coat. "And East Peak's ridge tie-line must have iced out in the pass."

Downstairs, opening the heavy storm door took both pairs of shoulders against the wind. Horizontal snow stung their cheeks like coarse sand. Twenty miles south beyond the hills, the sky over the city hung pitch-black without a single streetlight glow; out here in the creek basin, amber battery lanterns marked the footpaths toward the river trailhead.

Outside the upper rigging shed, eight people were already crowded under the eaves in headlamp beams, breath puffing white and fast. Two shouting voices cut sharp across the wind before Elara reached the steps.

Elias, who tended the root cellars and seed vaults, stood nose to nose with Mara from the South Fork refugee shelters, while veteran machinist Tobin stood beside an open breaker box slapping snow off his leather gauntlets.

"Read the discharge meter, Mara!" Elias yelled, snow caked white in his eyebrows. "With East Peak's line down in the blizzard and Creek One offline, the salt batteries won't hold forty minutes at full heater load! Drop the seed-vault temperature below thirty-four degrees and frost bursts every sprouted potato eye and grafting scion we saved for April! Cut the highway tent circuits now!"

"Cut heat to South Fork in nine-below wind and fifty people with city lung-rot don't wake up at daylight!" Mara shouted back, grabbing the front of Elias's coat with one mitten. "Twenty kids walked into Tent Six Tuesday with black frostbite on their heels! We aren't freezing sick children to save potato eyes!"

"Lose spring seed stock and three hundred people starve in October, Mara!" Elias yelled.

"And if the glycol loop in the machine bay freezes solid while you two scream at each other, it splits every lathe coolant jacket on the floor!" Tobin barked over both of them.

Elara stepped straight between Elias and Mara and pushed one gloved palm flat against each chest until they gave half a step.

"Save your wind," Elara snapped. "Both of you."

"Elara, the amp draw—" Elias started.

"I can read an amp gauge, Elias," Elara said, holding his eyes in the headlamp beam before looking at Mara. "We aren't pulling the breaker on Tent Six, and we aren't freezing the seed cellar. What does the gorge wire show, Tobin?"

Tobin blew hard into his cold mittens. "Heavy log strike knocked the intake grate loose ten minutes ago. De-icing strip shorted out on impact, anchor ice packed the bars, and the shaft's stalled dead."

"Then we go down the bank, pull the log, and get water turning again before the battery floor trips," Elara said, already unhooking a climbing harness from the pegboard.

Silas slung fifty feet of three-quarter-inch bull rope over one shoulder and handed Jace a five-ton chain hoist and two iron cant hooks. "Elara, Tobin, Jace, Mara, on me. Elias, go manually throttle cabin heat loops down to forty-eight degrees across the upper village to buy us twenty extra minutes on the battery bank. Move!"

Scrambling down the switchback gorge trail took ten minutes on hands and heels through wind-scoured ice crust. At the bottom, the narrows roared black and deafening between snow-plastered basalt walls. Chunks of white river ice the size of tabletops ground past in the flood current, slamming rhythmically against the half-submerged concrete nose of Turbine House One.

Wedged butt-first between the bent iron trash bars sat a thirty-foot windfall hemlock trunk, glazed in four inches of solid river spray that had cemented the intake gate shut.

Working by headlamp beams without wasting breath, Silas and Jace lashed twin anchor slings around two old-growth firs rooted in the bank rock while Tobin shackled the chain hoist between the tree bridle and the iced hemlock butt. Nobody owned drysuits; oiled wool and canvas gave a body four minutes in thirty-two-degree water before hands stopped obeying.

"Chain hoist won't pull that butt sideways while the gate latch is wedged under glare ice," Silas shouted into Elara's ear, pointing his light at the submerged side housing. "Somebody's got to wade out on a tether, chip the collar off the lower hinge spike, and pull the pin while we take strain on the log."

"My shoulders fit narrower between the wing walls," Elara shouted back, already clipping her waist harness onto the upstream safety line and checking the lanyard on a four-pound steel mash hammer.

Silas looked once at the black water churning past the concrete sill, checked the double figure-eight knot at her waist with his bare thumb and forefinger, and slapped her shoulder once. "Haul on your wave!"

Elara stepped off the shelf into the river.

Cold punched clean through two layers of wool and canvas in half a second, knocking the breath out of her throat in a sharp gasp. Thigh-deep black current tore sideways at her knees as she leaned upstream into the tether and jammed her hobnailed soles between slick cobbles. Ten feet to her left, Jace and Tobin waded knee-deep into the eddy spray together to hook the heavy iron grab-tongs deep into the frozen bark of the hemlock trunk before scrambling back onto the slick ledge beside Silas and Mara at the hoist lever.

Reaching the concrete cheek of the intake box, Elara plunged both arms past the elbows into black water. Within fifteen seconds pain stopped and her fingers turned to clumsy wooden pegs. Four inches beneath the surface, solid glare ice entombed the ring-head of the main iron hinge pin.

Unhooking the mash hammer, she took one lungful of freezing spray, ducked her head and shoulders completely under the roaring surface, and swung blind against the ice collar.

Water clamped around her skull like an iron band. Once, twice, three times she hammered steel against submerged ice, feeling dull shocks jar up her numb wrists. On the fifth blow the ice shell fractured. Dropping the hammer onto its wrist cord, she wedged stiff fingers into the split, wrenched two slabs loose from the iron eye-bolt, and broke surface gasping through clicking teeth.

"Pin's bare!" Elara yelled, hooking both wrists through the submerged iron ring because her fingers could no longer feel their own grip. "Take strain on the trunk!"

Up on the rock shelf, Silas, Jace, and Mara threw their combined weight onto the long cheater pipe of the chain hoist while Tobin worked the ratchet pawl, guiding the slack chain clear of the iced sprocket. Iron links groaned taut above the white water; wet bark shrieked as the hemlock butt eased two inches back off the trash bars.

"Pull it!" Silas roared over the chute.

Bracing both boots flat against the submerged concrete sill, Elara threw her back straight backward into her safety tether. Frozen iron screeched against its socket, hung for one sickening beat, and tore upward out of the lug.

Freed of its pin, the heavy trash grate swung wide. In the same breath Jace kicked the cant hook clear as the main current caught the loosened hemlock root-wad and rolled the massive tree tumbling down the white rapids.

Elara's boots swept clean out from under her in the backwash; Mara and Jace grabbed her harness webbing at the bank lip and dragged her chest-first onto the snowy gravel.

Behind them, forty tons a minute of black floodwater, carrying three submerged slabs of two-hundred-pound anchor ice that had been dammed behind the hemlock, shot full-bore down the open concrete throat straight into Turbine One's scroll case.

Deep inside the stone powerhouse, the shaft spun hard for four seconds. Up the gorge trail, two pole lamps flared bright white.

Then a sound like a twelve-gauge shotgun went off inside the cast-iron turbine scroll.

Every boot sole on the rock shelf jumped an inch as a second, ragged metallic crash tore through the powerhouse floor. Cast from recycled truck-axle carbon steel without Iron Creek's vanadium alloy to absorb freezing shock, two primary runner blades had snapped clean off at the hub under the anchor-ice impact and driven sideways through the outer cast-iron scroll casing.

Boiling brown river spray exploded sideways out the powerhouse doorway as the mangled runner jammed dead at six hundred revolutions, slamming a monstrous hydraulic water-hammer straight back up the intake chute.

"Penstock's gonna split!" Silas shouted as the buried forty-inch steel feed pipe up the slope boomed like a struck kettle drum and shuddered in its concrete saddles. "Drop the emergency headgate! Drop it now!"

Tobin was already lunging across the iced concrete cap to knock the dog-pawl out of the overhead iron sluice gate hoist.

Just as his hand hit the latch, a second back-surge slammed the half-lowered iron gate upward from underneath. Glare ice packed inside the cast sprocket teeth made the wet steel chain skip two links and whip violently backward across the iron housing lip.

Tobin didn't even have time to pull back. Heavy chain links snapped flat across his left hand, pinning three fingers square between sixty-weight iron chain and the cast-iron frame with a wet, sickening crunch.

Tobin let out one short, ragged animal cry and went down on one knee, trapped fast by the hand while pressurized water roared out the split powerhouse wall below them.

"Chain's got him!" Mara screamed.

Jace didn't hesitate a heartbeat. Snatching the four-foot solid iron pry bar off the snow, he jammed the chisel point straight into the whipping sprocket teeth ten inches above Tobin's trapped wrist, locking his tattooed forearms against the kicking bar until the tendons in his neck stood out like cords to keep the chain from taking Tobin's whole arm in.

Silas vaulted onto the cap beside him, hooked a second bar under the jammed link, and heaved upward with every pound in his back. Two inches of slack opened; Mara grabbed Tobin under both armpits and yanked him backward onto the snow as Silas kicked the release pawl clean out and dropped the eight-hundred-pound iron headgate crashing down into its sill slot.

With a deafening thud the main chute sealed shut. Diverted floodwater boiled harmlessly up over the stone side-spillway instead, leaving the cracked powerhouse dripping and dead silent under the wind.

On the trampled red snow at their boots, Tobin sat hunched double clutching his left wrist against his stomach. His split leather gauntlet hung in bloody ribbons; beneath it, the middle, ring, and little fingers of the finest lathe machinist in the valley were crushed pulp and white bone splinters.

"Belt!" Elara bit out through chattering teeth, already dropping onto frozen knees beside him and stripping her wet wool scarf free while Silas yanked his leather trouser belt off and cinched it hard above Tobin's left forearm to slow the bright red pumping onto the ice.

Tobin stared down once at his ruined hand, his face gray as putty under the headlamps. "Lathe chuck," he whispered stupidly through white lips before shock took his legs out. "Left my four-jaw chuck set up on—"

"Don't look at it, Toby, eyes on me," Jace said fast, kneeling behind the older man to haul one arm across his own shoulders while Silas took the other side. "Elara, flip the spillway paddle onto Auxiliary Wheel Two before the hill freezes! We've got him!"

Numbed past feeling from the knees down, Elara limped twenty yards across the spillway catwalk, broke the ice off the manual handwheel with a wrench, and spun thirty turns until creek water poured into the smaller secondary Pelton wheel built for summer low-water loads.

Inside the side shed, the small auxiliary generator whined up to pitch. Up on the ridge, emergency bulbs flickered back on at half-strength amber.

The penstock hadn't burst, the seed potatoes wouldn't freeze tonight, and Tent Six would keep minimal heat. Looking back into the main stone powerhouse where black water dripped steadily out of a jagged nine-inch split in the cast-iron scroll case, Elara already knew the winter math before touching her slate: Turbine One was dead until spring foundry pours, taking forty percent of the valley's winter generating power down with it.

\bigskip

Lin kept clinic lamps burning on surgical priority for three hours while snow piled four feet deep against the cedar walls. She saved Tobin's left thumb, index finger, and palm flap, scrubbing cast-iron grit out of crushed bone by magnifying loupe before stitching clean stumps across the outer three knuckles. By nine that morning Tobin lay awake and silent toward the plaster wall under willow-bark tincture, a thick white club of linen resting on a pillow beside his chest.

At noon, Elara posted the new winter ration schedule on the Commons Kitchen door. With forty percent of their hydro capacity shattered in the gorge, every residential cabin stove and heat loop dropped to a strict fifty-two degrees overnight; hot wash-water ran Tuesdays and Saturdays only; and the lower pottery kilns and timber dryers went onto split night-shifts whenever wind power dipped, forcing crews to work double turnarounds in thirty-eight-degree bays whenever the ridge blades spun. Nobody argued at the board, though faces around the soup tables looked pinched and hollow-eyed inside thick coats they no longer took off indoors.

Three mornings after the storm broke, under a brittle blue sky at fourteen below zero, nineteen-year-old Lia came sprinting down the packed snow lane from the radio shack without a hat on, slipping twice on the ice before catching herself on the kitchen porch rail.

"Elara! Silas!" Lia panted as they stepped out with tin mugs in hand. "Analog VHF emergency band, sixteen-fifty kilohertz! Open voice, no encryption!"

Inside the cramped radio room, grey static hissed across the bench speaker between ragged bursts of a hoarse voice everyone at the sawmill council two weeks ago had heard shouting from the back row.

"\textit{...repeat, any station receiving in the lower basin, this is Iron Creek Camp Upper... penstock wiped out in Tuesday's ice slide, spring box buried under forty tons of shale... diesel gen-sets gelled solid Wednesday night... got two dead in Bunkhouse Two and nineteen people down with black frostbite in the shaft tunnel... if anybody downriver still monitors open band, for God's sake send tracked rigs before dark...}"

Nobody in the tiny shack spoke. Having broadcast formal withdrawal notices and wiped their mutual-aid keys twelve days ago to hoard two tons of alloy steel, Harken's camp had possessed no automated distress cascade when the mountain came down on their single pipe, and no neighboring watershed had seen a single red warning flag until someone crawled up a ridge with a frozen car battery and an old marine transceiver.

Behind Elara in the doorway, Tobin stood with his bandaged left hand slung high against his wool vest, staring at the hissing speaker grille without blinking. For three full seconds Elara looked at those three missing fingers wrapped in bloody gauze, the exact price paid because Iron Creek's alloy ingots were still stacked uselessly in Harken's dark shed, and felt something cold and ugly stir behind her ribs.

Then Silas reached past her shoulder and keyed the brass desk microphone.

"Iron Creek, this is South Valley Base," Silas said flatly into the static. "Burn your bunkhouse furniture in metal drums inside the tunnel entrance and keep everyone awake. Two tracked snow-cats and a medic sled are leaving our gate in twenty minutes. Five hours to your pass."

\bigskip

It took eleven hours round-trip through chest-deep powder drifts above timberline.

Just before midnight, headlights swept across the frozen yard outside the clinic annex as both battered tracked carriers ground to a halt. Jace, Silas, and two plateau walkout nurses jumped down in stiff, frost-whitened parkas and lowered the tailgate ramps.

Nineteen huddled shapes wrapped in soot-blackened quilts stumbled or were carried on litters into the dim fifty-five-degree corridor of the annex. Two children had gray waxen cheeks; a woman clutched swollen purple hands wrapped in rags; and near the end of the line walked Harken, twenty pounds lighter than at the sawmill, leaning on a splintered pick handle because four toes inside his slit left boot had frozen solid black.

As Harken limped past the doorway where Elara and Tobin stood holding wool blankets out of quartermaster stores, the former mine boss looked up once, saw the thick white club bandaging Tobin's left hand, and dropped his eyes straight back to the concrete floor without uttering a word.

Nobody jeered at him, and nobody offered a warm speech either. Lin pointed the litters toward cots stripped from the study rooms, Jace went back out into the dark to shovel snow off the carrier tracks, and Elara walked down the cold corridor turning two more workshop breaker switches off so the clinic sterilizers wouldn't trip the auxiliary wheel.
