\chapter{Fracture Lines}

Aris opened his eyes on Thursday morning, forty-one hours after Lin drilled into his skull.

Inside Clinic Room Two, a small woodstove burned precious dry birch to hold the air at sixty-four degrees. Elara stood at the foot of the narrow iron cot with her knit cap twisted tight in both hands while Lin checked the clear plastic drain tube taped behind the thick white turban of gauze wrapping Aris's shaved head. Stainless surgical wire held his swollen left jaw locked teeth-to-teeth.

When Elara stepped gently into his line of sight on his left side, Aris's dark eyes focused on her coat collar, climbed slowly to her face, and widened with recognition.

"Hey, Aris," Elara said softly. "You're in the clinic. Lin's got you. Don't try to sit up."

Aris's left hand came up immediately off the sheet and gripped the iron bedrail hard. On his right side, however, his forearm lay curled inward against his lower ribs, fingers hooked stiff and trembling slightly against the unbleached cotton blanket. Four days ago those five right fingers had centered twenty-pound cylinders of wet stoneware clay on a spinning kick-wheel without looking down.

Behind his wired teeth, Aris's throat worked fast. His lips parted at the corner, breath hissing hard through his nose as he stared straight at his own motionless right hand on the quilt.

"Hhh," came out through the wire gap. Then nothing.

Panic flared sharp across his bruised eyes. He tried again, neck muscles straining above his bandage collar, left fist pounding once against the mattress ticking as his mouth shaped a sound that wouldn't cross from brain to tongue.

"Wa... k-k..." Aris choked out, saliva bubbling pink at the left corner of his mouth before breaking into a harsh, trapped sound deep in his chest. Hot tears spilled sideways out of his outer eye into the gauze over his ear.

Lin stepped in fast, laying one warm palm firmly over Aris's pounding left fist and tilting a plastic straw to the clean right corner of his lips so he could take two small sips of cold water.

"Swelling pressed hard against the motor and speech strip on the left side before we got the bone flap lifted," Lin said quietly once she stepped out into the chilly corridor with Elara and pulled the cedar door shut. Dark smudges sat deep beneath the nurse's eyes. "Drain's clear and he understands every word we say to him, Elara. Right leg has partial push, right hand has almost nothing, and he's got severe expressive aphasia. Maybe six months of daily speech and hand therapy brings thirty or forty percent back. Maybe it doesn't. He isn't throwing clay on a wheel again."

Elara leaned the back of her head against the cold earthen plaster of the corridor wall and shut her eyes for three breaths.

Before she opened them, heavy boots came fast up the stone entry steps. Silas pushed through the front doors with wet sleet crusted across his shoulders and snow still clinging to his beard.

"You need to come down to the lower timber yard right now," Silas said in a low voice, glancing once toward Aris's closed door to make sure nobody inside heard. "Vane walked out of the north perimeter cabin twenty minutes ago when shift watch changed."

Elara straightened off the wall instantly. "Walked out past the highway gate?"

"Walked straight down the creek path into Lodge Four," Silas said grimly. "Marek, Cole, and seven guys off the night kiln and sawmill crews met him on the porch, brought him inside by their woodstove, and wedged a two-by-four under the latch. Told Tarn when he came down the steps that nobody's putting Vane on supervised ditch detail until the uphill council answers for running sixteen-hour freezing shifts on the lower sheds while feeding outsiders."

\bigskip

By noon, a wet grey sleet was falling sideways across a valley split down an invisible chalk line.

At midday soup in the Commons Kitchen, half the long trestle benches sat empty; the lower timber and charcoal crews had drawn raw oats and salted fish in a single zinc bucket at eleven-thirty and carried it back down to eat behind closed doors at Lodge Four. Meanwhile, word of Aris waking up unable to move his potter's hand or speak his own name had run through the pottery shed, the water-line crew, and the greenhouse bays inside an hour.

When Elara and Miriam walked down the muddy wheel track to Lodge Four at two o'clock to serve notice for the afternoon lot-jury seating, Marek and two burly sawyers stood across the top porch plank with axes tucked head-down behind their heels, breath steaming in the sleet.

"Vane's got nothing more to say to your rigging hall, Elara," Marek said flatly, refusing to meet Miriam's eyes. Marek had helped raise the locust arches in Sector Seven back in September; today grey exhaustion hung like wet cement under his cheekbones, and split purple chilblains showed across his bare thumbs. "What happened to Aris is ugly as hell, nobody inside this door says different. Except your own winter board stuck eight men on double midnight stoking turns for forty straight days at thirty-eight degrees while Vera's electronics kids and basement clerks kept daytime hours! Now a man loses his head for five seconds in the cold and you want to parade him up and down the drainage ditches like a whipped mule? Not happening. Either Vane keeps bunk and regular timber shifts down here with us, or nine of us pack every crosscut saw and chain block in Shed Two onto flatbeds before Sunday and start our own camp upriver."

"Those saws belong to three hundred neighbors, Marek, not nine angry men behind a barred door," Elara said evenly from the bottom step, sleet dripping off her hood brim. "And five seconds of 'losing his head' left Aris in Room Two unable to lift his right fingers off a blanket or say the word water."

Marek winced half an inch around the eyes, then hardened his chin. "Lot jury opens at four in the mill if they want our logbooks. Vane stays behind this door till we hear fair shift rules."

He stepped back inside and dropped the timber bar into its iron brackets.

Walking back up the slushy hill beside Miriam, Elara heard Miriam's ash stick punching deep holes into the mud at every step.

"Draw the twelve jury names out of the brass drum right now in front of the whole kitchen," Miriam said quietly. "Skip every steward who sat Tuesday night, including me, Corin, Marta, you, and Silas. Neither side trusts an elder's chair today. And double watch on the clinic lane tonight."

Miriam wasn't wrong about nightfall.

At ten-fifteen that night, with sleet turning to thick freezing fog along the creek bottom and the fifty-two-degree curfew dimming cabin windows across the slope, the brass fire-triangle outside the carpentry shop began clanging wild, ragged strokes.

Elara and Silas were still awake in the basement routing room going over kiln shift logs with Gabe when the sound tore down the stairwell.

"Lower bridge!" Gabe shouted, pulling up the infrared gate feed on Screen Two where blurred figures carrying storm lanterns and long iron bars were marching fast down the muddy lane toward Lodge Four. "That's Doran and ten people from the pottery and water sheds!"

"Gabe, stay on the board and wake Miriam and Lin!" Silas snapped, already sprinting up the concrete steps two at a time behind Elara.

They cut straight down through the dark orchard terraces, boots sliding wildly in freezing mud, and burst out onto the packed cinder lane thirty yards short of Lodge Four.

Jace was already there alone. Coming off his evening compost turn, he had planted himself bare-headed in the middle of the narrow bridge planking with his empty, tattooed hands held chest-high in the lantern glare, backing slowly toward Lodge Four's porch as eleven furious neighbors pushed forward out of the fog.

At the front of the knot walked twenty-four-year-old Doran, Aris's bench partner since Year Two, carrying a three-foot steel tire iron in his right fist. Behind him marched six water-crew hands, three young teenagers from the coastal arrivals, and Harken's older cousin carrying pickaxe handles and oak cant-hook shafts.

Up on Lodge Four's covered porch twenty feet behind Jace, the heavy plank door banged wide open. Marek, Cole, and five kiln stokers spilled out under the eaves clutching splitting mauls and iron pokers, with Vane standing half-shadowed in the doorway behind Marek's shoulder.

"Step off the planks, Jace!" Doran's voice cracked high and raw across the sleet. "I just spent two hours holding a spit cup for Aris while he cried trying to say his sister's name! That bastard's sitting ten yards behind you drinking hot tea by a woodstove! Move aside, we're walking him past the cattle guard onto Route Nine right now in his socks!"

"Come down off those boards and try it, clay-boy!" Cole yelled back from the porch rail, hefting an iron firebox poker.

"Nobody touches those steps!" Elara shouted, sliding through the mud around the bridge post to plant her boots squarely beside Jace while Silas vaulted the low ditch rail on his left and Miriam hobbled fast down the gravel behind them, leaning hard on Gabe's arm.

"Out of the road, Elara!" Doran lunged sideways off the bridge planks through ankle-deep slush to get past Jace, swinging the steel tire iron up to shove Silas back.

Silas didn't step back and didn't throw a punch; he stepped straight inside the arc, caught the cold steel bar six inches below Doran's fist in both bare hands, and locked it dead down at waist height between them until both men's boots sank two inches into the mud.

"Let go of the iron, Doran!" Silas said between clenched teeth, six inches from the younger man's wet, shaking face. "Put one drop of blood on that porch tonight and we got nine bodies in the clinic by midnight and state troopers driving right through an open gate on Monday!"

"He crippled him, Silas!" Doran choked out, straining with both arms against Silas's grip while hot tears ran down his cheeks in the sleet. "You expect me to eat porridge across the hall from him for ten years because we're too high-minded for a county jail?!"

Jace stepped right up until his soaked linen chest touched Doran's raised forearm. Under the swinging yellow storm lantern, Jace yanked his left sleeve back to the elbow, shoving the raised white keloid slash running six inches from wrist to inner crook straight under the light.

"Look at that seam, Doran!" Jace said without yelling, his low, raspy voice cutting through the fog harder than a shout. "State prison yard at Monroe, Cellblock C, eight winters back. Kid stole two packs of tobacco off my bunk, so four of us righteous tough guys cornered him in the laundry room with mop wringers to teach him respect. Crushed his windpipe in ninety seconds. Next week his cousins gutted my bunkmate in the shower line with a sharpened paint scraper, and guards shot two more off the catwalk."

Around Doran, two of the teenagers with pick handles lowered their tips three inches toward the mud. Up on the porch, even Marek stopped shouting.

"Once you start settling accounts in the dark with iron bars, it never stops at one guy walking down the highway," Jace said quietly, eyes moving from Doran's wet face straight past the lanterns up to Vane standing stiff in the doorway. "And you up on those steps, hiding behind eight tired friends because you don't have the guts to sleep alone in a cold shack after breaking a kid's brain, you're pulling the exact cellblock coward play onto people who gave you a roof!"

Vane flinched back half a step into the shadow of the doorjamb as if struck across the mouth.

Miriam walked slowly past Silas's elbow until the iron ferrule of her ash stick clicked against the bottom wooden porch riser between both crowds. Her knitted shawl was soaked black with sleet.

"Twelve names came out of the brass kitchen drum at four o'clock in front of sixty witnesses," Miriam said into the hush. "Two barge girls, Bryn from the vans, Tobin's apprentice Leo, Sarah from the autumn highway intake, three orchard hands, a weaver, two green-crew plumbers, and Tarn. Nine o'clock tomorrow morning they sit in the shearing barn. Marek, your kiln shift logs go on the table first, every crooked hour of them. Right after lunch, Vane walks into Clinic Room Two with Lin and sits beside Aris's bed before a single word of judgment gets voted. Anyone carrying iron in this lane five minutes from now answers to that same twelve alongside him."

For ten long heartbeats, nobody moved except sleet hissing on the lantern glass.

Slowly, Doran's white fingers loosened on the steel tire iron. Silas took the bar cleanly out of his grip, tossed it with a heavy splash into the drainage ditch, and rested one broad palm for two seconds on Doran's heaving shoulder before letting go. Turning without another word, Doran and the ten figures behind him walked stiffly back up the dark hill into the fog.

Up on Lodge Four's porch, Marek looked sideways once at Vane standing silent behind the jamb, spat into the snowbank, and spoke down to Jace.

"Have two jury escorts here at eight-thirty," Marek muttered. "Door'll be unbarred. Nobody takes Shed Two's saws tonight."

"Jace and I stay right on that bridge bench till eight-thirty anyway," Silas said flatly. "Go put your mauls away."

\bigskip

Nothing about the next forty-eight hours looked like a pamphlet on neighborly harmony.

All Friday morning inside the cold shearing barn, the twelve randomly drawn neighbors went line by line through six weeks of winter power ledgers. Before noon they handed Elara and Silas a blunt rebuke to their faces: since January's turbine wreck, uphill scheduling clerks had left heavy night-kiln turnarounds falling disproportionately on the same fourteen lower-yard stokers without rotating electronics, clerical, or textile hands into the freezing bays. Starting Monday, every able-bodied adult under sixty, including Elara, Vera, and the radio clerks, went onto mandatory four-hour midnight stoking rotation once a week, and overnight kiln firing dropped by a third even if spring brick stock fell short.

At two o'clock Friday afternoon, Jace and Tarn walked Vane across the packed snow into Clinic Room Two.

Only Lin, Miriam, Doran, and two women from the lot-jury stood back against the plaster wall. Propped forty-five degrees on pillows under the skylight, Aris watched the door open with his left eye wide above the white jaw-wire.

Vane stopped four feet from the iron cot, his soot-stained cap crushed flat in both hands. All week down at Lodge Four he had talked loud about unfair amps and grabbed wrists; standing in bright afternoon daylight smelling boiled sage and antiseptic, looking down at the shaved, purple-stitched crescent curving above Aris's ear and the master potter's useless right hand twitching spasmodically against a folded towel like a landed fish, every practiced speech died dry in Vane's throat.

Aris stared straight back at him without blinking. Lifting his good left hand off the rail, Aris picked up an unfired clay pinch-cup Lin had set on his tray for grip exercise, placed it deliberately against his curled, trembling right fingers, and tried to close them around it.

His right thumb jerked sideways instead. The dry clay cup rolled off the blanket and shattered into four grey shards across the stone floor right against the toes of Vane's boots.

Aris didn't look down at the broken clay; he locked his dark eyes onto Vane's face and strained with everything in his chest against the wired jaw.

"Wh... wh..." Aris forced out, veins standing thick up his throat as tears of sheer fury leaked sideways into his bandage. "Wh... y?"

Vane stared down at the grey clay dust across his boot leather. Color drained completely out of his weathered cheeks; his throat clicked twice when he swallowed, and he couldn't lift his eyes back above Aris's blanket hem.

"Didn't—" Vane started in a hoarse scrape, caught Miriam's cold gaze across the bed, and shut his mouth tight. Hands shaking slightly on his wool cap, he turned toward the door without finishing the excuse.

\bigskip

Saturday afternoon the twelve-person jury posted its binding terms on the kitchen door, signed by eleven of the twelve:

Vane remained permanently barred from all kilns, workshops, and unsupervised crews; he moved permanently into the single north woodlot cabin under two-person rotating accompaniment; and for two full years, reviewed quarterly only with Aris's signed consent, six days a week of his heavy timber-splitting and ditch labor were credited directly to supporting Aris's household and full-time speech and physical recovery. Seven of the nine lower-yard stokers from Lodge Four accepted the overhauled shift schedule and returned silently to the retorts Monday morning.

Two did not. Cole and a sawyer named Garrett packed their personal duffel bags Sunday at noon, signed individual exit receipts at the western gatehouse without shaking Elara's hand, and walked down the county highway toward the river towns swearing they wouldn't live under uphill hypocrites. Two hours later, Doran's older cousin and his wife packed their own cart and walked out the exact same gate in the opposite direction, telling Miriam coldly at the cattle guard that any town willing to feed the man who broke Aris's brain under the same sky wasn't a home they wanted to raise their son in.

That Sunday night in the Commons Kitchen, barley stew steamed hot in the copper pots and nobody went hungry, yet an ugly, jagged quiet hung between the trestle tables. Pottery and water hands sat clustered near the south greenhouse glass; lower kiln workers ate fast near the vestibule coat hooks with eyes on their bowls; and out by the north woodlot half a mile upslope, Jace pulled his sheepskin collar up against the March wind and sat down on an overturned stump outside Vane's dark cabin window to begin the midnight-to-four watch.
