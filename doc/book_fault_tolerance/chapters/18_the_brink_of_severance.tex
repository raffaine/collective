\chapter{The Brink of Severance}

Three weeks into April, the east wind smelled of burned insulation and wet coal ash.

Elara stood on the upper catwalk of the south solar ridge with one forearm braced on the galvanized pipe rail. Below her boots, green orchard terraces stepped down toward the swollen creek, where Foundry crews were finally pouring high-alloy sand castings from salvaged mine scrap to rebuild Turbine One before next winter. Forty miles east beyond the foothill saddle, however, dirty yellow-brown smoke hung flat across the coastal plain.

Over the winter, the old metropolis had stopped unraveling in slow legal filings and started coming apart at the bolts. Four outer suburbs had defaulted on municipal water bonds since February; fire engines sat up on blocks without diesel while electrical fires burned whole commercial avenues down to brick chimneys. Wealthier hillside enclaves above the bay had ringed private diesel microgrids and hydroponic warehouses with concertina wire and contracted guards, leaving miles of dead strip malls and dark apartment blocks between them to precinct extortion crews and copper strippers.

And twelve miles east of Elara's fence line, smoke rose straight up from the Blackwood Siding rail spur too.

Boot steps clanged steadily up the steel stair grating behind her. Silas stepped onto the landing carrying two tin mugs of chicory tea, iron filings glinting dull gray in his beard from grinding road-barrier lugs down at the Foundry.

"Dawn watch just got back off the east saddle," Silas said, handing her a mug and leaning both elbows on the pipe rail beside her. "It wasn't county deputies down by Blackwood Siding last night."

Elara looked up sharply from her tea. "Who was it?"

"Meridian Asset Protection," Silas said flatly. "Unmarked slate-gray six-by-sixes unloading off flatcars right inside Brenner's shop fence. Looks like once we pulled Blackwood's clearing keys in November and their grain ran low in February, Brenner cut a deal with Meridian's regional board: diesel drums and corporate scrip rations in exchange for staging rights on his rail spur and copies of the old utility trench maps into our creek."

Elara's stomach went cold. "Out-of-state contractors."

"Every one of 'em," Silas said. "Two pickups from their scout detail tested our north sheep gate at four-thirty this morning with a winch cable. Gabe and Tarn ran them back down the gravel with flare shells and air horns, except they weren't rattling fences to steal mutton. They were measuring culvert widths for heavy axle loads."

"City emergency council signed the Energy Reclamation Directive yesterday afternoon," Elara said quietly, looking east toward the brown smudge above Blackwood's spur. "Declares any non-grid generating station over fifty kilowatts within sixty miles of the metro boundary subject to immediate state-marshal seizure. Meridian's gas peaking plants are cannibalizing their own turbines for spare bearings; they want our hydro house and ridge panels intact, or smashed so nobody else gets ideas."

Down at the base of the solar mast, the brass signal horn gave two short blasts followed by a long low note: scheduled tight-beam window in the mill cellar.

"Priya's mirror link is up," Silas said, checking his wrist slate.

Walking fast down the gravel switchbacks through the village, they passed welding teams stitching salvaged highway I-beams into Czech-hedgehog tank traps outside the tractor barn while greenhouse crews bolted winter polycarbonate storm shutters back over glass panes they had unbolted only ten days ago. Across the lane in the schoolhouse yard, old Corin sat on an upturned apple crate counting river pebbles with eight five-year-olds while keeping one eye on the eastern sky.

Down in the vaulted stone cellar beneath the textile mill, six salvaged monitors threw pale green light across fieldstone walls. Maya stood at the center table with three-week-old Clara asleep in a woven cloth sling knotted snug across her chest; Gabe stood at her elbow tuning the rooftop laser receiver.

On the middle screen, grainy black-and-white video resolved into Priya's narrow, soot-smudged face beneath the concrete ribs of an abandoned storm-drain junction.

"Fifteen minutes before direction-finding vans box our roof mirror, Elara," Priya said without greeting, voice hoarse through static pops. "They raided Fourteenth Street Depot Thursday morning. City tactical squads plus two platoons of Meridian contract guards with battering rams. Smashed the tilapia tanks with sledges and zip-tied nine kitchen kids."

Gabe leaned both palms hard on the table edge. "Did you lose the medical stores?"

"Lost four empty steel vats and a chalk sign, Gabe," Priya said, showing her teeth briefly without humor. She tilted her slate camera twenty degrees left: behind her in the tunnel, thirty teenagers in headlamps were strapping high-output propane-biogas burners, ceramic water filters, and trauma kits onto heavy electric cargo trikes. "Voted two weeks back to abandon fixed addresses altogether. Set up soup pots and wound dressing inside a dead church basement or subway mezzanine for ten hours, feed eight hundred neighbors, then roll three wards west before tactical vans finish fueling up. Better yet, since the water authority shut public standpipes on the east side, whole tenement blocks stand roof lookout for our bike teams with flashlights."

A high two-tone warble sounded off-screen behind Priya's shoulder.

"Scanner van just turned onto Sixth," Priya said fast, reaching for the lens cover. "Watch your east rail flank, Silas. Four engine plants along the river corridor went out on wildcat strike Monday when bosses tried locking machinists inside overnight; workers dumped cold water into hot slag pits before private guards cleared the bays. And check shortwave off the interior interstate. Something big is rolling west-to-east toward the coast."

Static ate the window.

Silas pulled the logged shortwave transcript folder onto the left screen. Across three neighboring farm counties to the south, ten-thousand-acre corporate soy and corn estates had strung electrified razor wire around grain elevators while county bailiffs seized independent farmers' ditch pumps on bank liens.

"Whole state's splitting into company stockades and starving road camps," Silas muttered.

"Except the company stockades don't own the clerks anymore," Maya said quietly, shifting one hand under Clara's sleeping back as she keyed an amber overlay across the county map.

Elara stepped closer to read the labels. Dozens of small amber triangles sat over county courthouse basements, regional water-board pump stations, and state highway maintenance garages.

"For four years Kael's legal circle didn't just file tax forms, Elara," Maya said. "Every time county employees came out to our farm stand for clean flour or insulin from the apothecary network, we built quiet ties. Nine days ago the governor's energy desk ordered District Four Water to drop the headgate feeding our upper canal. District chief hydrologist's sister lives at River Landing Two; he reclassified our intake ditch as a federally protected salmon-spawning bypass, locking the shutoff inside an eighteen-month review docket. Yesterday morning when state police motor-pool requisitioned six lowboy trailers to haul concrete jersey barriers up Route Nine, the shop foreman in Duvall red-tagged every tractor in the yard for cracked brake drums."

"Working people letting the air out of the tires from the inside," Silas said slowly.

"Problem is Meridian knows half the county civil service won't swing the hammer for them," Elara said, thinking of the slate-gray six-by-sixes unloading inside Brenner's fence at Blackwood Siding. "Which is why they brought out-of-state contract muscle in by rail."

Before Maya could answer, the cellar's overhead lamp cut to pulsing red and a low three-note chime sounded from the western ridge laser link: a cryptographic handshake Elara hadn't heard chime live in six years.

Maya bent fast over the verification log, fingers flying across the mechanical keys.

"Not a spoof," Maya said, looking up wide-eyed at Elara. "Signed with two founding root keys off the West Pass line-of-sight dish."

Four lines printed clean across the center glass:

\begin{center}
\textbf{WEST PASS GATE: THREE-RIG CONVOY AT MILEPOST FOUR}\\
\textbf{ROOT KEYS VERIFIED: KAEL / JAX}\\
\textbf{ORIGIN: INTERIOR HIGHWAY CORRIDOR}\\
\textbf{REQUESTING IMMEDIATE GATE CLEARANCE}
\end{center}

Nobody spoke for two seconds.

"Three thousand miles across the interior," Silas said quietly. "If those two came off the plains now, the middle of the map is on fire."

"Get to the west gate before the hydraulic rams open," Elara said, already turning for the stone stairs. "Maya, keep Clara down here in the cellar till we see what's behind their dust."

Outside in windy April sun, two hundred residents were already forming a watchful half-circle inside the western earthwork gate while herders drove sheep into the stone lower barns and Tarn's crew rolled welded steel tire-spikes beside the guardhouse. Built of double-stacked shipping containers packed solid with river cobble and overgrown with thorny Himalayan blackberry and perennial hop vines, the heavy gate leaves groaned outward on greased hydraulic rams.

Pale alkali road dust rolled across the cattle guard off County Road Nine.

Out of the dust ground three battered diesel rigs burning waste vegetable oil that smelled like scorched fry-grease. Behind two tarped flatbeds carrying salvaged battery banks and lathe beds rolled an eight-wheeled articulated heavy hauler plated around the radiator and cab doors in rusted quarter-inch Corten steel, an angled highway snowplow welded across its front frame rails and its tall tires crusted white with Nevada salt and eastern ash.

Fifty feet inside the earthworks, air brakes blew off with a sharp hiss and the big six-cylinder clattered dead.

Ticking hot manifold iron filled ten seconds of quiet before the heavy steel driver's door creaked open.

Kael climbed down off the top step slowly, one boot at a time, stiff in the hips from sixteen hours behind the wheel.

Ten years ago, when he had handed thirty-two-year-old Elara an encrypted thumb drive in a rainy transit shelter downtown, Kael's beard had been dark brown; today it was almost entirely iron-white, his tall frame pared down to dry leather and tendon inside a canvas coat mended at the cuff with braided copper wire.

Across the cab, the passenger door swung open and Jax dropped straight off the high running board onto the gravel. Wire-thin, sides of his skull shaved close with dark braided hair tied back in a loop of green ground-wire, he wore a faded coverall under a rigged chest vest stuffed with spectrum analyzers, fiber cleavers, and a worn nine-millimeter pistol riding high under his left arm. Before his boot heels settled, Jax's dark eyes had already checked the solar ridge sightlines, the welded hedgehogs by the barn, and Tobin's bandaged hand at the edge of the crowd.

Elara walked four paces out from the half-circle with Silas beside her.

"Kael," Elara said, her throat unexpectedly dry in the wind. "Jax."

Kael stopped three feet away, squinting past the dust on his lashes at the gray threads in Elara's hair, the terraced orchards climbing green behind the mill roof, and two hundred fed people standing in mended wool behind her.

"Still standing, El," Kael said, his voice hoarse from road dust. Reaching out, he gripped her forearm briefly with dry, cracked fingers, equal to equal, before nodding once to Silas. "Damn scarce sight east of the Rockies right now."

"How bad were the plains?" Silas asked plainly.

Jax hauled a dented aluminum pelican case out of the cab footwell and set it down hard on the front bumper.

"Half the prairie nodes we seeded four years back are gone, Silas," Jax said without softening a syllable. "Two basins in Kansas dried clean out when aquifer pumps failed and fuel militias burned their granaries; another up on the Missouri got shelled flat by National Guard artillery under an emergency grain-requisition decree in January. Don't let anybody sell you bedtime stories that the old system's dying peacefully in its sleep. We burned two transmissions getting west of Boise to beat the interstate lockouts."

Kael turned half around so the whole gate yard could hear him over the wind in the blackberry canes.

"Forty-eight hours from tomorrow morning, an emergency joint session of the state legislature and appellate bench convenes under closed martial rules in the capital," Kael said. "We pulled the draft docket off a clerk's courier line in Spokane. It's called the Emergency Reclamation Act. In one omnibus writ, they're voiding every Social Purpose Corporation charter, agricultural land-trust deed, and private microgrid exemption across this basin, classifying autonomous settlements as unlawful hoarding cartels."

Murmurs rippled low across the gravel yard. Up near the barn wall, Gabe shifted sideways until his shoulder stood in front of the cellar stairwell where Maya and Clara were waiting.

"And they aren't waiting till next month to serve it," Elara said, pieces clicking coldly together in her head. "Meridian unloaded sixty private security contractors onto Blackwood Siding's rail spur twelve miles east of here last night."

Jax snapped his fingers once, dark eyes flashing. "Brenner's rail yard. Wondered where their forward depot sat. State patrol provides the lawful badge out front so regular troopers don't feel like highwaymen, and Meridian's hired heavy-hitters ride right behind them to crack skulls if county boys hesitate."

"Once that court voids our corporate shells Friday morning, every stalling filing we've used for seven years turns to ash," Silas said quietly.

"Which is why we burn the shells ourselves tomorrow morning before their clerk even gavels in," Kael said, opening the aluminum latches on the bumper case to lift out a thick oilskin-wrapped leather folio. "Ten years ago we built corporate masks to buy growing room. Scaffolding's done its job. Tonight we file voluntary dissolution on every dummy company in this valley, and at eight o'clock Friday morning in federal-state appellate chambers, Tahoma and twelve river-basin tribal chairs walk in beside me to assert direct co-stewardship over this entire watershed under Article Six of the unextinguished 1854 Sovereign Point Elliott and Medicine Creek Treaties."

Silence dropped across two hundred people at the gate.

"Federal treaty law sits above the state constitution and above state eminent domain," Elara said slowly, following the gambit straight to its edge. "If the valley lands are formally enrolled under tribal watershed co-management with forty county water engineers swearing affidavits that our farms keep three counties out of famine, the governor's seizure writ collides head-on with federal treaty jurisdiction."

"Buys you a courtroom fight they can't rubber-stamp in ten minutes," Jax said bluntly, slapping the fender. "Only catch is timing. Meridian's column out of Blackwood Siding is gonna roll at daybreak Friday before the appellate judges finish reading page two. If their bulldozers get inside your gate and smash the Foundry and hydro house first, no court paper on earth puts broken cast iron back together."

"And if anyone on our side fires a rifle at state patrolmen on County Road Nine," Silas said, looking straight at the pistol holstered under Jax's arm, "governor declares armed insurrection, federalizes two brigades with helicopter gunships, and treaty law goes straight out the window."

Jax looked at Silas for three quiet seconds, then unbuckled his holster rig, cleared the chamber onto his palm, and tossed both gun and belt onto the passenger seat of the cab before slamming the steel door shut.

"Your road, big man," Jax said evenly. "Tell me where you want concrete poured."

Elara turned around to look at the people standing inside the earthworks: Tobin with his three-fingered left hand tucked inside his belt; Doran standing two yards away from Marek with neither man looking away; Miriam and Corin leaning on their ash sticks; Gabe standing tall at the cellar steps. Winter had already taken fingers, speech, Olander's breath, and three households off their roll. Nobody standing in this gravel today thought survival came free.

"Lia, open all basin channels," Elara called across the yard. "Tell Tahoma's council rigs we meet their elders at the boundary line before Friday dawn, and tell Priya's bike teams in the city we need every downtown intersection jammed with soup pots when the precinct riot vans try rolling west. Silas, Tarn, fire Furnace One. We cast road blocks through the night and pull every last switch on the municipal pylon."

She looked back at Kael's road-hollowed eyes.

"Get some hot stew in you first, Kael," Elara said quietly. "You've got sixty miles of back roads left to make court by morning."
