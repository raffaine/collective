\chapter{Ignition and Echoes}

When Elara and Silas pushed out through the Foundry's heavy canvas thermal curtains after midnight, the sleet had turned to thick, wind-driven snow. White drifts were already banking against the slag carts and burying the wheel ruts down toward the highway.

Neither of them spoke walking up the cinder path past the banked charcoal retorts. Six hours of haggling over kilowatt-hours and herring barrels on top of four sleepless days in the mud had scraped them both clean out of small talk. Snow hissed sharply wherever flakes touched the hot iron stack collars. Ahead of them, Silas walked with a slight hitch in his left stride where the pole-transformer arc had burned through his wool seam that afternoon, dark smoke-grease still crusted behind his ear.

At the foot of the outside iron stair climbing the old textile mill to Silas's loft, Elara's numb boot missed the frost-slick second tread.

Silas's hand closed fast around her elbow before her knee hit the grate, holding her steady against the gust coming down the alley.

"Watch the rime on the nosing," he said, his voice rough from five hours of foundry smoke.

Elara didn't step back when she got her footing. Under the single amber bulkhead lamp above the landing, snow caught in his eyebrows and dark beard. For three years they had kept an unspoken half-arm's length between them in every workshop and council hall, careful never to tangle personal hunger into shared work while fifty households depended on them staying level-headed. Tonight, looking up at the burn mark along his coat and the exhausted lines cut deep beside his eyes, she didn't have the strength left to step back onto her own tread.

Silas didn't let go of her sleeve either. Turning the iron latch at the top of the stairs, he shouldered open the insulated plank door and drew her inside out of the wind.

The small loft under the mill rafters was warm from hot-water return pipes looped beneath the pine floorboards from the dye-house boiler below. Mycelium wall panels hushed the gale outside down to a dull rush, and the room smelled of boiled linseed oil, dry cedar shavings, wool grease, and iron filings from the neat rack of chisels over Silas's bench.

Elara dropped her snow-stiff coat onto the peg by the door. When Silas shrugged his left arm out of his singed jacket, his breath hissed sharply between his teeth; he caught himself with one palm against the corner of the workbench, color draining under the soot on his cheekbones.

"Sit on the bed," Elara said, crossing the narrow rug in two steps. "Let me see your side."

"Just flash through the seam," Silas muttered, though he didn't move away when she reached for the heavy brass zipper of his coveralls.

"Sit down, Silas."

He sat heavily on the edge of the low timber bedstead. Elara worked the stiff brass teeth down past his sternum and eased the scorched canvas off both shoulders. Across his collarbones and forearms lay old pale scars from twenty years of steel and line work, and low along his left ribs a palm-sized patch of skin stood angry red and blistered where copper flash had punched through wet wool.

At the washstand she poured cold pitcher water over a clean linen square, picked up a tin of yellow calendula-and-honey salve from the shelf, and knelt between his boots.

"Cold water first," she warned quietly.

The moment wet linen touched raw skin to lift the soot out, every muscle across Silas's stomach and ribs locked hard; he gripped the edge of the mattress plank without making a sound.

"Almost clear," Elara murmured, dabbing lightly instead of wiping.

"Keep going," Silas said, his voice low and tight. Looking down at the top of her head, his thumb brushed once against the edge of her collarbone where her flannel collar gapped. "Keeps me in the room."

Elara paused with the open salve tin in her palm and looked up. His face was ten inches above hers in the dim glow off the stove grate.

"Where were you up on Pole Four this afternoon?" she asked quietly. "Right after you clamped the jumper, before you climbed down."

Silas looked past her shoulder at a dark pitch knot in the floorboards. Under the floor, hot water ticked softly through a copper elbow.

"South Precinct Substation Twelve, down in the metro," Silas said after a long silence. "Seven Novembers back, right before I walked out. Freezing chemical rain coming sideways off the bay, forty miles an hour. I was working high-voltage tower crew for Meridian Grid."

Elara warmed two fingers of salve between her palms and laid it gently across the red burn along his ribs, letting him talk at his own speed.

"District management had cut rigging budgets three quarters running," Silas said, eyes still fixed on the pine knot. "Swapped forged steel carabiners out for cheap die-cast zinc-alloy clips from an offshore liquidator and called it supply-chain optimization. Marcus, a twenty-four-year-old kid I'd trained for two years, was out on the wet cross-arm beside me jumpering a cracked porcelain bell before evening peak."

Silas's fingers dug into the rough wool blanket on his knees. "His boot kicked out on wet frost. Primary lanyard took his weight for half a second, then that cheap cast clip snapped clean across the gate. Sounded like a twenty-two going off in the rain."

Elara's hand stilled warm against his unburned ribs.

"I got both hands around his secondary tail before he cleared the lower brace," Silas said, so softly she barely heard him over the wind on the roof. "Wet three-strand poly. Burned straight through soaked elkhide gloves and took the skin off both my palms before I could snub a wrap on the angle iron. Felt the tail whip out of my fingers. Two hundred and ten feet straight onto concrete."

He looked down at her then, eyes dark and dry and wide awake. "Walked into division safety the next morning with my hands wrapped in gauze and dropped the two halves of that cast clip on the superintendent's blotter. Nobody in the room would say Marcus's name out loud. Company counsel had already flagged the incident number under 'unforeseeable weather slip' and mailed his wife a credit voucher for ninety days of residential kilowatt service. Ninety days of lightbulbs for a twenty-four-year-old kid's spine."

His chest rose hard once beneath her palm. "Every time a pole pig screams in sleet like today, I feel wet poly burning out of my grip. Swore if I ever strung wire again, no bean-counter behind glass was ever going to decide whose life penciled out."

Elara reached up with her clean hand and rested her palm flat along his unshaven jaw, her thumb rubbing a dry smudge of carbon off his cheekbone.

"Nobody fell today, Silas," she said quietly. "Every kid down at South Fork has heat tonight because you stayed on that cross-arm."

Silas closed his eyes and leaned the heavy weight of his head into her palm, letting out a long, ragged breath through his nose.

Rising off her knees between his boots, Elara pressed her forehead against his temple, breathing in scorched wool, pine resin, and salt sweat.

"You don't have to hold the pole up in your sleep too," she whispered against the corner of his mouth.

She kissed him first, slow for half a breath and then hard and open-mouthed as three years of biting back every touch in crowded kitchens and forge bays gave way at once.

A low sound came out of Silas's throat. Both hands came up around her waist, careful of the salve on his left side yet pulling her straight onto his lap before standing and lifting her up against him. His beard scraped rough across her chin and throat; his mouth was hot, tasting of bitter chicory tea and salt. Elara tangled both hands into the damp hair at the nape of his neck and kissed him back until neither of them had breath left, backing blindly half a step until his calves hit the timber rail and they went down together across the wool blankets.

Elara fumbled at the horn buttons of her own damp flannel shirt, shoving wet wool and thermals off her shoulders because another layer of cloth suddenly felt intolerable. Yet the instant Silas's warm, rough palm slid bare across her ribs to pull her flush against his chest, a trapdoor gave way inside her own memory, cold fluorescent light slicing right across the dark loft.

\textit{Twelfth floor, Municipal Resource Allocation.}

Six winters ago, sitting in dry wool slacks behind dual monitors that smelled of toner dust and lemon sanitizer while sleet ticked against triple-paned city glass. She hadn't worked two hundred feet up in freezing rain like Silas; she had worked in a heated cubicle where people froze cleanly by row number.

\textit{Account Cluster 442-B: 92 Days Delinquent. Execute Automated Steam-Valve Lockout? [Y/N]}

Through a grainy thirty-second hardship-appeal window in the corner of her monitor, a woman in her twenties had held a coughing two-year-old wrapped in a winter parka up toward a cracked phone camera, white breath puffing visibly inside a dark kitchen as she begged for seventy-two hours until a delayed warehouse check cleared.

And Elara remembered her own eyes flicking up to the yellow probationary metric blinking beside her employee ID. One more manual winter deferral dropped her below ninety-four percent compliance and revoked her transfer out of collections, the transfer she needed to keep Gabe's pediatric coverage intact. Tell yourself the supervisor two desks down will just approve the batch at five o'clock anyway. Tell yourself you don't own the boiler.

Her index finger had clicked \textit{Confirm} with a tiny, polite plastic click.

\textit{Valve Lockout Queued.}

She had never learned whether that toddler made it to March. She had sat on the clean end of the exact machine that dropped Marcus onto substation concrete.

A harsh, dry sob wrenched straight out of Elara's ribs before she could bite it down, her whole body jerking once beneath Silas's weight.

Silas checked instantly, propping himself up on both forearms above her, eyes wide in the stove light. "Elara, did I lean on—"

"No," she gasped, hooking both arms hard around his neck and dragging him back down against her, pressing her face into the hot hollow between his jaw and collarbone so she wouldn't have to see white fluorescent glass in the dark. "Don't stop. Please don't pull back from me."

Tonight she couldn't say \textit{Cluster 442} out loud, not yet; she only needed skin, weight, and the living heat of him to scour six-year-old office frost out of her marrow.

"Stay right here, Silas," she whispered against his pulse, voice shaking. "Please."

Silas didn't ask a single question. Shifting his weight off his burned left ribs onto his right forearm, he slid one broad hand beneath the small of her back and pulled her up against him skin to skin, kissing her damp eyelids, her jaw, then her mouth until her shivering turned into a completely different kind of tremor.

Restraint burned clean off them both. Elara kicked the last tangle of canvas trousers free of her ankles and locked her heels behind his thighs, nails digging hard into the heavy muscle across his shoulders as he pushed inside her with a low, broken groan against her ear.

Nothing gentle or tentative stayed between them; the timber bedstead creaked rhythmically against the pine wall as Elara arched up to meet every slow, driving thrust, mouth open against his throat, tasting salt on his skin while heat built low and blinding in her belly. Outside the rafters a winter gale tore across dark miles of broken highway, yet inside the blankets there was only the slick friction of two tired, weathered bodies refusing to stay cold a minute longer, holding tight onto each other as the tension coiled intolerable and broke through them both at once in ragged, muffled cries against each other's shoulders.

Afterward, neither moved for a long time. Chest still heaving, Silas rolled onto his unburned right side, keeping one arm tucked under her neck and pulling two heavy grey wool blankets up over their slick backs against the draft off the floor.

Under the pine planks, boiler return water gurgled quietly on its night loop. Down at South Fork three miles south, forty wall tents hummed warm on highland current; forty miles north in the crags, Harken's dark pass sat sealed under three feet of fresh drift.

Silas's thumb moved slowly, absentmindedly back and forth across the curve of her hipbone.

Elara stared at the dim orange chink of coals behind the stove damper until her breathing finally slowed to match his. Nothing outside this room was settled: the alloy pack train wasn't coming, Creek Turbine One had a hairline fracture waiting under black water, and state marshals were still counting roof tarps along Route Nine. Still, as Silas pressed his chin lightly against the crown of her damp hair and fell asleep first, her eyes finally stayed shut without seeing a glowing city screen behind them.
