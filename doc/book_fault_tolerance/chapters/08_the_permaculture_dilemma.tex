\chapter{The Permaculture Dilemma}

Three days of cold November rain had turned every footpath between the cabins into ankle-deep gray mud. Inside the Commons Kitchen, eighty people stood shoulder to shoulder in wet wool coats, steaming near the two cast-iron woodstoves while rain drummed steadily on the corrugated roof. Nobody had brought out a fiddle tonight, and the cider urns sat dry on the sideboard.

Elara stood beside the butcher-block prep table at the front of the room with clay caked halfway up her bootlaces, turning a ten-inch length of clipped high-tensile pasture wire over in her fingers.

"It is a replication of the exact violence we left behind," Julian said from the middle of the floor. His voice shook slightly at the start, then found its pitch. Before coming up the valley three years ago, Julian had spent a decade running loading docks at a corporate hog-processing plant outside the city, and he rarely raised his voice unless pushed. "We talk every week about breaking chains of extraction. Yet three hundred yards from this hall, we breed sentient animals as components in a nutrient machine and tell ourselves a pastoral bedtime story so the winter slaughter goes down easier."

Along the west wall, a dozen younger apprentices and recent city arrivals murmured low agreement. Over the autumn they had started calling themselves the Abolitionists during supper-table arguments, pushing to phase out the valley's sheep, goats, and ducks altogether.

Across the stove aisle, Marta unfolded her arms. Sixty-two years old, with wet gray hair tucked under a knitted cap and mud dried in the seams of her canvas bibs, she had managed the valley's pastures since the first winter. Silas and Jace stood two paces behind her alongside the rest of the farm and compost crews.

"You're looking at a hillside paddock and seeing your old loading dock, Julian," Marta said flatly. "There isn't a conveyor belt or a stun chute within forty miles of this creek."

"A small slaughterhouse is still a slaughterhouse," someone called from near the dish racks.

Marta took one step into the aisle, heavy boots creaking on the fir planks. "Then let's talk dirt instead of slogans. When we signed the land trust on these four hundred acres, the upper flats were dead hardpan, burned out by seventy years of anhydrous ammonia and herbicide runoff. Dust in August, brick in January. How do you think we grew eighty tons of grain and roots off it this summer without buying a single bag of petrochemical nitrate from the city?"

She pointed toward the dark, rain-streaked windows. "Hooves and rumen. Two hundred sheep and goats rotated across tired grass, breaking capped crust, dropping manure that wakes up dormant soil fungi, and ducks clearing slugs out of the ditch beds before blight sets in. Pull the stock off those slopes, and within two growing seasons our yields drop by half."

Beside her, Jace cleared his throat, rubbing a thumb across the faded ink on his wrist as half the room looked over. He didn't like speaking in big halls, and kept his eyes fixed on the stove pipe rather than the crowd.

"Tried running four test windrows behind the kilns all summer without barn bedding, just chipped alder and kitchen scraps," Jace said quietly. "Pile never cleared a hundred and ten degrees. Weed seeds survived right through it, and the winter squash we planted in that batch came up stunted yellow. You want hot compost by April without diesel turners or factory chemicals, we don't have another way yet."

Shouts broke out from three corners at once. Elara rapped a hardwood rolling pin sharply against the edge of the prep table until the noise dropped back under the drumming rain.

"We didn't call an emergency floor at nine at night to settle agricultural theory," Elara said, holding up the clipped length of galvanized wire so the lantern light caught the bright diagonal shear marks on both ends. "We're here because somebody took bolt cutters to the east paddock at two this morning."

Silence settled fast.

Whoever had clipped three spans of wire and left an unsigned note reading \textit{NO MORE CAGES} pinned to the gatepost had expected twenty goats to bound off into the mountain timber. Instead, cold rain and the scent of a coyote pack up on the ridge had turned the herd straight downhill into the unfenced winter root beds.

Elara had walked down with a headlamp at five-thirty that morning. Twenty hungry goats had spent four hours trampling and chewing their way down twelve raised rows of storage carrots, parsnips, Lutz beets, and hubbard squash: nearly four weeks of cellar rations for the lower lodges, churned into icy brown soup.

"Who cut the wire?" Elara asked quietly.

Rain hissed against the stovepipe collar. Near the back pantry door, nineteen-year-old Lia stepped half a pace out of the crowd. She had come inland six months earlier from a flooded coastal tenement and worked mornings in the radio room; her parka hem was still dripping puddle water onto the floorboards, and her chin trembled as she lifted it.

"I did," Lia said. Her voice cracked once, then steadied. "Every morning walking up to the repeater shack, I had to look at those wether kids by the salt trough knowing Marta already had four of them marked on the whiteboard for December culling because the hay barn's short. That's the exact spreadsheet math my old landlord and every city agency ran on us, deciding who fits the winter budget and who gets cut loose. Somebody had to break it."

Marta rubbed two fingers hard between her eyebrows. She didn't shout; she walked four steps down the aisle and stopped across from the girl.

"Do you know what coyotes do to a tame dairy goat in wet timber, Lia?" Marta asked, keeping her voice low. "They don't die quick, and they don't live free. And because they had more sense than you did last night, they stayed out of the trees and ate two tons of your neighbors' January supper instead."

"So we just accept killing because the math's convenient?" Lia's eyes shone wet in the lantern light. "Where does that stop?"

Julian stepped sideways until his shoulder stood between Lia and the front table. "We aren't nineteenth-century peasants, Marta! Breweries in the old metro zones grew precision protein in stainless vats without touching a single animal—"

"On megawatts of coal and gas grid power, using refined corn dextrose trucked across three states!" Silas cut in, his temper flashing for the first time all evening. "Have you looked at our hydro gauge in January, Julian? We barely pull enough winter current to keep the Foundry from freezing and run the clinic autoclaves! Where are you getting sterile stainless vats, replacement seals, and forty spare kilowatts when the county's already flying camera drones over our transformer pad?"

"Better lean rations than blood on the porridge!" someone behind Julian yelled back.

"Easy to volunteer lean rations when your kids aren't the ones coughing in a cold cabin!" Tarn barked from the foundry bench.

Elara stepped off the low hearth platform right into the narrow gap between Julian and Silas, holding both palms flat at chest height until Tarn sat halfway back down.

"Look out that north window," Elara said, pitching her voice low enough that people had to stop shouting to hear her. "Two county drones filmed our fence line yesterday, and outside contractors tried to blow a three-ton furnace melt before lunch. Nothing would make the county prosecutor happier by February than watching half this valley walk out over moral purity while the rest of us go short on calories."

She looked straight at Julian and Lia. "Nobody who ever smelled a commercial packing plant thinks you're crazy for hating cages. If we ever start talking about living stock like factory tonnage, we deserve to lose this place."

Then she looked down at the dried gray mud crusted across her own knuckles from trying to dig unbroken beets out of the trench at dawn.

"Nobody gets clean hands by buying food in plastic trays, and nobody gets clean hands here either," Elara said. "Field plows kill voles and ground nests. Apple sprays kill caterpillars. Someday they'll lay every one of us in the upper timber compost to feed black locust roots. Living in the dirt means owing your life to other living things and paying attention to what it costs."

She turned half toward Lia without stepping closer. "Cutting that fence didn't spare a single kid goat, Lia. It dumped four weeks of missing winter roots onto eighty households who trusted you with the keys to the tool shed."

Lia stared down at her wet boots, one hand clamped tight around her opposite elbow as two tears slipped off her chin. "I didn't think they'd go into the garden," she whispered. "I thought they'd head up the creek."

"Goats go where the food is," Marta said dryly, though some of the iron had gone out of her posture.

Elara looked around the packed kitchen. "Nobody's getting thrown off the land, and nobody's losing tool keys forever. Tomorrow at first light, farm and kitchen crews salvage whatever broken roots we can scrub and ferment into sauerkraut crocks before rot sets in, and I'll message the upriver settlements to see what surplus turnips or barley we can trade for before snow closes the pass."

She fixed Julian and Lia together. "For the next four weeks, both of you rotate off your regular bays onto morning stock duty with Marta and Jace. Mucking the loafing shed, checking hooves for rot in this mud, stringing temporary netting, and standing beside Marta when winter culling comes due. If you still want to argue pasture policy at solstice council after you know every animal in that shed by sight, the floor's yours."

Julian pressed his lips thin. He looked down at Lia's bent head, then across the aisle at Jace and Marta's mud-caked bibs, and gave one short nod. Lia wiped her nose on her coat sleeve and whispered, "Okay."

"One thing goes the other way too, Marta," Elara added, turning back to the older woman. "Take the culling tally off the kitchen corridor whiteboard. And sit down with Julian and Lia this week to see whether there's any way to move that herd through the woods without locking them inside shock wire."

Marta blew out a slow breath through her nose, tipping her chin half an inch toward Julian. "Five-thirty at the lower barn, both of you. Wear tall boots."

Little by little the crowd broke up, neighbors pulling damp wool collars up around their ears as they stepped out into the pouring dark by twos and threes. Near the doorway, Silas paused long enough to tap two knuckles lightly against Elara's upper arm.

"Held together," he murmured.

"Until December, maybe," Elara said, sliding the clipped wire into her coat pocket. "Go get five hours' sleep. We're digging mud at dawn."

\bigskip

By daybreak the storm front had pushed east, leaving thick white valley fog hanging low through the alders. Walking down the gravel lane with a stone crock crate balanced on her hip, Elara heard pitchforks scraping concrete inside the goat shed before she could see the roof.

Through the open Dutch doors, Marta and Jace were showing Julian and Lia how to roll urine-soaked straw out of the corners onto two wheelbarrows without spearing the packed clay floor underneath. Nobody was making warm small talk; Julian's shoulders looked stiff in his borrowed oilskin vest as he leaned into a heavy forkful, and Jace silently kicked a wooden ramp plank straight so Lia's barrow wheel wouldn't bog in the threshold rut.

Around their boots, twenty goats wandered out into the misty mud yard, shaking wet ears. A flop-eared speckled doe walked straight up to Lia and butted her knee twice looking for salt. Lia froze, rested her muddy glove briefly between the goat's horns, and bent back over her fork.

Elara hitched the crate higher on her hip and kept walking down toward the ruined carrot beds.
