\chapter*{Epilogue}

\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Epilogue}

Nine months after the saws cut the aluminum trunk lines at Milepost Twelve, a deadlocked, exhausted United States Supreme Court let the appellate treaty ruling stand in a three-page unsigned order.

Nobody in the valley mistook those three pages for a change of heart in Washington. By January, federal tax receipts had fallen below debt-service interest, two eastern port authorities were trading grain directly in kilowatt-scrip, and the Interior Department simply lacked the fuel, cash, or reliable regiments to wage a ten-front war against the river basins still growing winter wheat. Like every empire before it when the ledger ran dry, the old state didn't repent; its reach just shrank back toward the capitals it could still garrison.

Two autumns after County Road Nine, up on the windy gravel turnout above the west gate, Kael and Jax were strapping tarps down over crates of heirloom barley seed, ceramic filter candles, and open-board radio repeaters on the back of their eight-wheeled truck.

The Corten armor plates stayed bolted to the cab doors. Out beyond the Cascade passes, the world hadn't turned into a garden song.

"Got overnight packet burst off the Gulf relay before we pulled the dish," Jax said, cinching a ratchet strap tight with both hands and squinting against the bright October glare. "Port commune outside Galveston went dark Tuesday. Petrochemical junta out of Houston rolled artillery barges up the ship channel when refinery diesel ran out. Maybe four hundred folks got out up the Trinity River barges; rest of the docks burned."

Kael paused with a coil of hemp rope over his forearm, looking east past the foothill saddle where thin cooking smoke rose above the old metro plain, cleaner than two years back, though half the outer sprawl remained boarded-up concrete shells contested by salvage syndicates while Priya's neighborhood blocks painstakingly jackhammered parking lots into fenced potato beds one ward at a time.

"And the Upper Platte watershed?" Kael asked quietly.

"Still split three ways since last summer's drought," Jax said, checking tire pressure with an iron gauge. "Half their upstream nodes hoarded canal water like Iron Creek tried here, collapsed by August, and now the downstream towns are absorbing two thousand dust-bowl walk-ins on half rations. Gonna take fifty years, Kael. Maybe eighty. Whole bioregions are gonna miscalculate or get crushed under falling masonry before that old carcass finishes rotting."

"Fifty years was always the real number, Jax," Kael said, climbing stiffly up toward the cab step before looking back down across the creek basin below them. "Nobody promised us every seed takes root. Only takes a few deep ones surviving winter to re-seed a burned slope."

Even twelve miles east along the rusted tracks at Blackwood Siding, the lesson sat plain in the weeds. Meridian Grid had pulled its flatcars and diesel drums out forty-eight hours after losing the Road Nine injunction, leaving Brenner's machine yard stranded without power or allies; by last November Brenner himself had drifted south, and the remaining fourteen machinists had spent a humble year trading repaired tractor axles on outer-probation terms just to earn winter oats back from the valley silos.

Five hundred feet below the ridge turnout, October sun lay warm and pale gold across four hundred acres that had held.

Inside the open Dutch door of the machine-shop tool crib, Tobin leaned his elbows on the half-shelf while checking out Olander's zero-to-one-inch steel micrometer to a fourteen-year-old girl from the highway arrivals. Clamping the wooden sign-out clipboard smoothly against his ribs with the healed two-fingered grip of his left hand, Tobin tapped the calibration log with his right pencil.

"Bring it back with a single speck of lathe swarf on the spindle threads and you sweep bronze chips till Solstice," Tobin told her mildly, watching her nod twice and trot off toward the milling bay.

Down the gravel path under the sunny south eaves of the pottery shed, twenty-eight-year-old Aris sat straddling a low bench beside a modified kick-wheel Doran and Silas had rebuilt with a heavy foot-pedal centering guide. His right forearm rested strapped loosely to a padded leather brace on his thigh to steady the tremor; using only his strong left palm and wet thumb, Aris drew a thick cylinder of dark creek clay slowly upward into a wide, slightly uneven soup bowl.

Lia paused by the drying racks with an armful of split alder kindling. "Glaze kiln's running Thursday morning if those dry in time, Aris."

Aris eased the flywheel to a stop with his left boot heel, looked up into the sun with the pale crescent scar curving clean above his left ear, and took four unhurried seconds to line the words up behind his tongue.

"Twelve... bowls," Aris said, slow, thick around the wired jaw scar, and completely clear. He tapped the wet rim once with his left knuckle, half a crooked grin lifting the good side of his mouth. "Hold... stew."

"Twelve's plenty," Lia smiled, setting the kindling down gently by the door before heading upslope, passing two hundred yards below the north woodlot trail where Vane still worked silent six days a week splitting winter cordwood beside Tarn's afternoon watch: never eating in the main kitchen hall, never forgiven by Doran, yet fed, warm, and alive under kept word.

Fifty yards lower in the terraced comfrey beds behind the clinic, Elara knelt on a folded grain sack tucking straw mulch around perennial roots while Silas worked ten feet down the trench replacing a cracked brass drip-union with a cast-iron T-joint poured out of their rebuilt Foundry.

Above the open collar of Silas's faded flannel shirt showed the thick white ridge where Dan Miller's baton had snapped his collarbone on County Road Nine; two miles down the lane, Miller himself now drove a timber tractor three days a week for River Landing Two after turning in his state badge that same afternoon.

"Creek One's new vanadium runner is holding dead smooth at eighty-eight percent load," Silas said, giving the iron fitting a final quarter-turn with his pipe wrench and wiping red pipe-dope off his thumb. "Even if January drops to twenty below again, nobody's freezing in the pottery bays this winter."

Elara sat back on her heels, flexing stiff dirt-caked fingers in the late sun, grey hair now outnumbering dark along both temples at forty-four. Looking across the comfrey leaves at Silas kneeling solid and unhurried beside the water pipe, she didn't feel triumphant or finished. Ditches still silted up after every hard rain, Kael's truck horn had just sounded twice in farewell heading back out into a breaking continent, and winter wood tallies still kept her up past ten on Sundays. What remained was a deep, quiet steadiness in her chest that hadn't existed two years ago.

From the grassy knoll above the upper apple rows, a high, clear peal of toddler laughter carried down the slope.

Elara and Silas both looked up.

Two-year-old Clara was tearing barefoot through late red clover in an unbleached linen smock, dark curls bouncing wild as she outran Gabe by six paces up the rise while Maya walked laughing behind them with a basket of windfall Jonagolds balanced on her hip.

At the crest of the knoll, just inside the spot where four severed utility cables lay overgrown by wild blackberry beside the quiet asphalt of County Road Nine, stood a waist-high cairn of rounded river cobbles and a young red cherry tree planted in its second autumn leaf.

Clara skidded to a stop in the clover beside the cairn, tipping her head all the way back to point one muddy finger up into the branches.

Hanging from the lowest bough on a loop of tarred hemp twine, turning slowly and dipping its sanded wings whenever the October breeze came up the gorge, rocked a small, sun-silvered cherry-wood bird.

Gabe caught up on the slope, breath hitching slightly where cold mornings still pulled at his healed rib-cage, and rested two fingers briefly on the warm top river stone of Jace's cairn before scooping his laughing daughter high onto his shoulder.

Down by the Sector Seven depot, the salvaged bronze bell rang three slow, unhurried notes calling the afternoon shifts in to wash up for supper. Silas stood up from the water pipe, held one muddy hand down to pull Elara to her feet, and walked beside her up the stone terrace steps toward the tables under the trees.
