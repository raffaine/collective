\chapter{Learning and Unlearning}

The diagnostic bay in Duvall Hall smelled of pine rosin, hot copper, and stale coffee. At sixteen, Gabe was all long wrists and restless knees, hunched over a wooden teaching bench with one eye pressed to a stereo microscope. Beneath the ring light sat a fingernail-sized current-monitoring board for the Foundry's main induction coil.

He wiped a smear of flux off his forehead with his sleeve, leaving a shiny streak above his eyebrow.

"Your ground joint is cold, Gabe," a dry voice said behind his left ear.

Gabe jumped half an inch, pulling the soldering pencil clear before the tip touched the trace. Master Vera stood over his stool with a ceramic mug in one hand. Vera was fifty-four, crop-haired, with reading glasses hung on a braided cord around her neck and tiny pale solder burns freckling the backs of both wrists.

"I was trying not to cook the security chip," Gabe said, setting the iron in its wire cradle. "Run the iron too hot and the tamper fuse blows and wipes the signing keys."

"And leave a dull, grainy joint like that and the contact resistance climbs," Vera said, tapping the schematic on her slate with a fingernail. "Resistance climbs, voltage drops. Voltage drops, the controller reads twenty percent less current than the furnace is actually pulling, assumes a ground fault, and dumps the main breakers in the middle of a three-ton steel melt. Re-flow it. Quick in, quick out."

Gabe dabbed a speck of fresh rosin onto the pad, waited two breaths for the iron tip to come back to temperature, and touched the joint. Dull gray tin slumped instantly into a bright, concave fillet around the pin.

At the next bench over, Gabe's friends Sam and Leo had the cracked white shell of a salvaged county traffic drone spread across a rubber mat. Sam was probing the flight-controller pins with a logic analyzer while Leo sorted machine screws into muffin tins. Like Gabe, they split their weeks hauling slag carts at the kilns and sweeping woodchips in exchange for bench hours with Vera.

"Clean?" Gabe asked, sliding off the stool so she could look.

Vera bent to the eyepieces, turned the focus knob a quarter-turn, and straightened up.

"Passable," she said. "Pot it in epoxy and flash the board. I want it bolted onto the Foundry feeder before noon."

Gabe grinned at the bench mat and reached for the ribbon cable.

"Utility sweep again?" Sam asked from the next table, looking up from the gutted drone. "I thought the legal filings stalled them last Tuesday."

"Filings stall county clerks," Vera said, walking over to the wall monitor where local radio relays glowed across a contour map of the creek basin. "Someone higher up hired outside network contractors this week. Yesterday afternoon the Foundry gateway dropped fifty thousand fake work requests in forty seconds."

"Trying to lock up the job queue?" Sam asked.

"Trying," Vera said. "Every outside packet has to post a small power-credit bond before the router even reads the header. Their dummy key burned through its bond on request number four, and the outer gate blackholed the other forty-nine thousand nine hundred and ninety-six. Still, whoever ran it was probing response times. Gabe, leave the epoxy to cure and bring your slate into the rack room."

Gabe followed her through the heavy insulated door into the server corridor. Built from welded bed-frame angle iron bolted straight to the concrete slab, the racks held rows of stripped municipal blades, open-board routers, and printed drive cages cooled by salvaged radiator fans.

"Sit," Vera said, pointing at a steel desk with a battered mechanical keyboard. "Today you run your final qualification drills. First problem: suppose an outside contractor splices hardline into an orchard repeater box tonight and spins up two thousand phantom member keys to stuff tomorrow's watershed vote. Run the flood script in the sandbox."

Gabe pulled the keyboard closer, opened the isolated test environment, and fired the script. On the right-hand screen, two thousand freshly generated cryptographic identities began casting simultaneous ballots to shut down the irrigation pumps.

"Watch the tally column," Vera said.

Gabe leaned forward. Although every phantom key carried valid math, none of them had ever co-signed a work shift with an existing neighbor, eaten at a kitchen terminal, or received an in-person voucher from two stewards in different guilds. On the graph, all two thousand accounts clustered together on an isolated branch with zero path weight back to living residents.

"Weight reads zero point zero," Gabe said as the status bar flashed amber, then green. "Quarantined the whole batch. Ballot total didn't move."

"Math is cheap; years of showing up for dish duty aren't," Vera said. "Second drill. Suppose they stop trying to vote and instead slip a malformed orchard-survey file into the shared repair queue to crash the parser. Pull file seven from the red folder and throw it at the test gate."

Gabe inspected the dummy file first: nested inside the elevation table was a recursive loop meant to eat every megabyte of memory on whatever server opened it. He hit enter.

The cooling fans in the rack didn't even pitch up. On the left monitor, a three-line notice rejected the upload before execution.

"Bounced at the boundary," Gabe read aloud. "Field four exceeded thirty-two characters, so the gate dropped the packet unread."

"Keep the templates strict and dumb, and half their clever tricks hit brick," Vera said. She checked her wristwatch. "Forty-five minutes off screens, rule's the rule. Go get some sun before your eyes lock at eighteen inches."

Outside in the Sector Seven courtyard, heat radiated off the concrete apron beneath the broad leaves of an old fig tree. Gabe dropped onto a plank bench beside Sam while Leo tossed a bruised windfall Gravenstein from hand to hand before biting cleanly into the good side.

"Vera pass your board?" Leo asked around a mouthful of apple.

"Passed the solder check, plus two sandbox drills," Gabe said, tipping his head back against the warm brick wall and closing his eyes against the glare. "My neck feels like wire."

"Saw two white utility quadcopters hovering just outside the property markers above the creek road twenty minutes ago," Sam said, wiping lithium grease off his thumbs with a shop rag. "Staying fifty feet over the county easement so they're technically legal, pointing long lenses straight down at the Foundry transformer pad."

"Let them take pictures," Leo said, tossing the apple core into the compost bucket by the steps. "South ridge solar is pulling peak kilowatts and the salt batteries are topped off."

"They aren't taking postcards," Gabe opened one eye to squint toward the eastern ridgeline. "Vera said outside contractors hit the Foundry router yesterday."

Before Sam could answer, the brass horn bolted under the depot eaves let go with three short, flat blasts, paused two seconds, and sounded three more.

Gabe's slate buzzed hard in his cargo pocket. Red text scrolled down the lock screen: \textit{GATEWAY SPOOF ALERT: FOUNDRY INDUCTION FEED TWO.}

"Speak of the devil!" Sam was already up and running for the steel door. "They're hitting the kiln line while a melt's running!"

Gabe and Leo sprinted on his heels down the concrete hall and back into the diagnostic bay.

Master Vera stood braced over the main standing terminal, both hands flying across the command prompt.

"Gabe, bench three!" she barked without looking around. "Someone's beaming directional microwave packets straight into the rooftop weather relay and injecting falsified thermocouple spikes onto Furnace Two's safety bus!"

Gabe slid onto his stool and pulled up the live Furnace Two telemetry. On the upper plot, the outer bridge reported coil jacket temperatures climbing past eight hundred and forty degrees Celsius, ten degrees shy of triggering the emergency quench trip.

"If that trip fires mid-melt, three tons of steel freeze solid inside the crucible!" Gabe said, opening the raw sensor list. "It'll crack the whole brick core!"

"Cross-check the physical current clamp on the busbar!" Vera snapped.

Gabe pulled up the hardwired hall-effect clamp bolted directly around the copper feed bar inside the Foundry basement, the exact sibling of the board curing on his mat. While the spoofed radio packet screamed eight hundred and fifty degrees, the copper busbar current sat flat as a ruler at four hundred and ten amps, and the cooling-water return line hadn't risen half a degree.

"It's a lie!" Gabe called out, highlighting both traces. "Busbar draw is dead flat at four-ten and return water's fifty-two Celsius! You can't gain three hundred degrees in six seconds with flat amps!"

"Sign the physical override and sever the rooftop bridge!" Vera ordered.

Gabe plugged his apprentice key fob into the side slot, bound the basement current trace and water-return delta into a fault proof, and sent it to Vera's queue. Vera slapped her master key onto the reader half a second later to co-sign.

On the wall display, the rooftop weather relay flashed red as the controller severed the wireless bridge and locked Furnace Two strictly onto its hardwired basement loop. Three inches shy of the emergency trip line, the temperature trace snapped back to five hundred and ten degrees. Down the hill, the deep sixty-cycle hum of the Foundry coils kept running without a stutter.

"Outer flood's starving out," Sam reported from bench two, his voice high and fast. "Whoever hired that microwave rig just burned their last relay token. Packet rate's down to zero."

"Leo, check the ridge camera," Vera said, leaning both palms on the desk edge.

Leo pulled up the telephoto Mast Three feed. "Both white quadcopters just banked east toward Highway Twelve. No smoke from the Foundry stacks, no breaker trip to write a citation on."

For ten seconds nobody spoke over the drone of the rack fans. Gabe looked down and noticed his right heel was bouncing rapidly against the rung of the stool; he pressed his boot flat to the concrete to stop it.

Vera walked over, looked at the signed fault log on his monitor, and pulled her key fob out of her pocket.

"Plug yours back in," she said.

Gabe slotted his key into the reader. Vera tapped hers beside it and typed a two-line endorsement into the guild register. Across the room, the solenoid on the master parts locker gave a sharp metallic clack, its indicator LED shifting from amber to steady green for his badge.

"Apprenticeship signed off," Vera said, picking up her cold coffee mug from the corner of his mat and making a face after one sip. "Clean up your flux jar before supper, steward. Starting Monday you're on rotating watch."

Sam punched Gabe once on the upper arm hard enough to sting, and Leo grinned from ear to ear. Gabe unplugged his newly endorsed key, switched off his soldering iron, and began capping the rosin bottles one by one.
