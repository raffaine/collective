\chapter{The Politics of Collapse}

The east wind coming over the ridge no longer smelled of pine duff or wet slate. Thirty miles down the basin, the coastal metropolis was burning whatever would catch, and the afternoon thermals carried the flat, sour reek of melted PVC, stale diesel, and scorched copper wire straight across the timberline.

Elara stood on the plank deck of Watchtower Two with a pair of scratched marine binoculars pressed to her eyes. Beside her at the oak rail, Silas had his coat collar turned up past his ears, mud dried gray up to the ankles of his greased boots.

Through the eight-power glass, brown haze lay trapped beneath the November inversion layer across the lower plain. Over the past six weeks the municipal grid had stopped failing by neighborhood and started failing by district; blacked-out tower blocks stood cold beside smoking commercial strips where fires had burned themselves out for lack of water pressure. Eight-lane highway viaducts that used to move ten thousand refrigerated trailers a week sat empty save for stalled cars pushed onto the shoulders and long dark strings of figures walking north along the breakdown lanes.

"Southside treatment plant went dark," Silas said, squinting bare-eyed against the glare at the river mouth. "That black column near the rail yards isn't house lumber. Somebody's burning tire piles near the port, and the substation by the bridge just blew another oil bushing."

Elara lowered the binoculars, cold brass stinging the skin under her eyebrows, and pulled back her wool cuff to check her forearm slate.

Across the e-ink map, their bioregional network looked nothing like a county chart with a courthouse in the middle. Hundreds of small creek farms, timber camps, dock cooperatives, and neighborhood machine shops overlapped in tangled rings of watershed pacts, tool shares, and clinic circuits so dense nobody had ever drawn the whole thing on one sheet of paper. Right in the middle, their own creek valley showed steady amber. Along the southern highway approaches, however, red request markers were stacking up faster than the dispatchers could clear them.

"Four hundred and ten people walked up to the South Fork road stations yesterday," Elara said, scrolling down the intake log. "Soup crews emptied every kettle twice and sent families toward the county fairgrounds shelter over the spur. Word came back an hour ago that the fairgrounds flooded its latrines and state troopers locked the chain-link gates at gunpoint. Every soul they turn around on Route Nine is going to walk straight back up our grade before dark."

Silas looked down from the horizon. Soot from Tuesday's furnace repair still darkened the creases beside his mouth. "We don't have the carry for thousands, Elara. Even after fermenting the trampled carrots from the east garden, our root cellars sit nine days above bare March rations. Open the lower gates to an unchecked rush and we eat next spring's seed potatoes by New Year's."

Elara didn't argue the math; she had spent the last two nights awake in the basement running bushel and kilowatt curves until her eyes blurred. In their shared ledger, local survival floors weren't up for polite debate. Once stored grain or battery reserves hit the winter survival line, automated cutouts dropped workshop feeders and external transfers to keep cabin stoves and clinic sterilizers running.

"Nobody's talking about letting crowds trample the seed sheds," Elara said, watching a thin gray smear drift higher above the distant highway cut. "Still, if twenty valleys bolt their gates while families freeze in the road ditches outside, we're no different from the gated subdivisions up on Bellevue Hill. Only difference is we wove our own coats."

"Coats don't stretch across ten thousand backs," Silas said quietly. He tapped the oak rail once with his mitten. "Flatbed's charged at the lower shed. Half the regional speakers are already parked down at the crossroads mill."

Three miles down the river junction stood the cavernous timber-and-iron shell of the old Weyerhaeuser sawmill, abandoned thirty years back and patched over time into neutral ground for watershed assemblies. Crews had laid two big brick rocket-stoves at either end of the concrete slab and stuffed them with dry alder splits, though thirty feet overhead the November wind still rattled loose tin sheets along the monitor roof.

Nearly ninety delegates sat on rough-sawn fir benches arranged in a wide oval, smelling of whichever country they had driven out of that morning. Near the west stove sat Thorne and the speakers from the Highland ridge fellowships, sheepmen and wind-turbine mechanics whose oiled wool smelled of wet snow and pine tar. Behind Thorne's bench sat Harken and three stone-faced foremen from the high Iron Creek mining and timber camps near the pass, their heavy canvas coats dusted with gray rock flour. Across the slab sat the Riverine barge-landing crews smelling of pike smoke and willow silt; Malik and four Coastal Port machinists with salt crusted on their cuffs and rags wrapped around cracked knuckles; Priya and five exhausted organizers from the Urban Retrofit blocks who had ridden electric cargo bikes out through the city's dead streetcar tunnels before dawn; a cluster of prosperous orchardists from the Northern Plateau; and Brenner from Blackwood Siding, the old rail-spur machine yard out on the dry eastern flats.

Elias, oldest of the central creek farmers, walked out to the middle of the sawdust circle and struck a hung truck-rim twice with a ball-peen hammer. Iron rang clear up into the trusses.

"We'll skip roll call," Elias said, his hoarse voice carrying across the concrete. "Everybody drove past the highway columns getting here. City water pressure failed below Fortieth Street on Sunday, state relief depots closed their gates at noon today, and seven times our October estimate hit the southern way-stations since Monday morning. Floor is open."

Alder knots popped loudly inside the east firebox.

Thorne stood up first from the Highland bench. Tall and spare in a mended sheepskin vest, her gray hair cropped short above pale eyes, she governed a string of rocky ninety-day-season valleys where late May snows killed careless orchards.

"On behalf of twelve Northern Ridge fellowships, we are posting notice under the Local Floor clause," Thorne said, her voice flat and unhurried.

Nobody murmured; every apprentice learned that clause before signing their first workshop card. No assembly, trade ring, or emergency vote could compel a settlement to ship out food, fuel, or medicine once its own winter survival floor was threatened.

"Effective six this morning, our pass gates closed to unscheduled foot traffic," Thorne continued, looking levelly around the oval. "We have pulled our winter barley and hydro margins off the open clearing board until April thaw. I advise every watershed in this building to lock its surplus lines tonight before panic on the highway drags the whole region down into the ditch with the city."

From the row behind her, Harken leaned forward on his knees. "Iron Creek and Upper Crag say amen to that, and go one better," the mine foreman called out harshly. "Why dance around half-measures? Any camp with sense ought to file formal exit papers tonight, sever outside clearing accounts completely, and keep every ingot and cord of fuel inside its own canyon till spring."

"You're talking about watching families freeze twenty yards outside our cattle guards," Priya said sharply from the city bench, dark hollows bruised deep under her eyes. "My block kitchens on Twelfth Avenue fed nine hundred kids oatmeal thin as dishwater this morning! We aren't asking you to import two million strangers into your sheep barns, Thorne. Give our urban blocks dry grain, water-filter candles, and surgical gauze, and we can organize neighborhood triage right inside the apartment shells so half those families never take to the highway in the first place!"

"Put hot porridge out on Twelfth Avenue on Monday and fifty thousand starving people will tear your cellar doors off by Wednesday," Brenner barked from the eastern rail-spur bench. Small-eyed and thick through the neck, he slapped his thigh with his cap. "Out at Blackwood Siding we've got freight tracks leading straight out of the metro yards to our shop fence. Yesterday two hundred stragglers tried camping under our water tower. My shop committee didn't wait for no sixty-day cooling-off clock or eighty-percent double ballot; we padlocked the siding gate at sundown and turned off the outer standpipe."

Dead quiet dropped across the center benches.

Elias lowered his hammer slowly to his side. "You shut water off at an outer sanctuary tap without an eighty-percent household vote, Brenner?"

Brenner's chin came up, color mottling his neck. "Easy for river farmers to quote founding parchment! Our diesel's down to forty drums and we feed our own lathe crews first!"

"Cross the sanctuary floor by committee decree and the review panel strips your network keys inside a week, Brenner," Malik said coldly from the Coastal bench. "Don't come crying to the harbor machine shops when your switch engines throw a rod." He held up one oil-bandaged hand toward the rest of the circle. "Same time, Thorne isn't wrong about raw volume. Down on the sound, unpaid coast-guard crews already tried boarding two of our kelp barges looking for diesel. Broadcast an open invitation to the metro basin and ten thousand desperate souls hit our piers by Sunday night. Our transformers would melt inside an hour."

On the bench beside Thorne, two orchard stewards from the Northern Plateau leaned their heads together over a slate; Elara caught one of them muttering about raising local orchard cover-crop reserve numbers before midnight and posting \textit{No-Transit} markers on the ridge grade so refugee vans couldn't even cross their gravel roads.

Elara stood up from the central bench. Drafts off the cold concrete bit straight through her damp wool stockings.

"Nobody in this mill is proposing we tear our fences down or raid anyone's children's grain," Elara said, pitching her voice straight across the sawdust ring to Thorne and Harken. "Look two months past December before you start cutting lines, though. Harken, what happens in February when rock-drill armatures burn out at Iron Creek and your camp has no copper winding wire from Priya's salvage crews? Thorne, whose kilns supply the orchard char your thin mountain dirt needs in April, and whose Foundry pours replacement bearing races when winter gales crack a turbine shaft on East Peak?"

Thorne didn't blink. "We carry spare stock for one winter."

"And what about the winter after that, once every bridge between us is burned?" Elara stepped out onto the concrete apron, pulling her slate off her forearm. "The city thirty miles south didn't collapse because people got hungry; it collapsed because every company and suburb hoarded behind private walls until the whole supply web snapped! Lock ourselves into fifty little mountain bunkers tonight and the state receivership courts will pick us off one valley at a time come spring."

She walked to the freestanding slate-board near Elias and clipped her cable lead onto the projector terminal. A stark chalk-white schematic lit the plywood screen: staggered roadside intake stations five miles apart along the county approaches, each capped at forty beds.

"We meet the road column in small, metered pockets before it bunches into a mob," Elara said. "Forty people per station: hot soup, delousing, frostbite care from the medics, and clear two-way agreements to join supervised forestry, salvage, or ditch-clearing crews before moving inland by tens and twelves across twenty watersheds. Spread across all ninety settlements in this hall, no single granary drops within ten percent of its safety floor."

"And state troopers?" called an orchardist from the Northern Plateau, arms folded tight. "Put forty-bed camps along Route Nine and the governor's emergency marshal declares an unlawful assembly and seizes every van with a valley stencil on the door!"

"Don't stencil a single banner on them," Elara answered immediately. "We already hold thirty separate agricultural labor co-op charters, county brush-clearing registrations, and salvage hauling licenses across three counties. Paper every roadside camp as an ordinary seasonal contract crew under dull corporate filings. State agencies know how to raid one big protest camp; they choke on thirty boring tax IDs filing routine compliance forms."

Harken spat into the sawdust beside his boot. "Clever lawyer tricks won't put porridge in a miner's belly when ten thousand city mouths show up anyway. Iron Creek isn't signing a single pound of ore or cordwood into highway soup pots."

Thorne stood up again, her shadow stretching long between the brick stoves. "The founding compact leaves every watershed sole judge of its local floor, Elara. Northern Ridge passes stay shut. Furthermore, if central valleys attempt to draw on shared regional emergency pools for highway camps without unanimous highland consent, our twelve fellowships will pull our relay repeaters off the spine and close clearing channels altogether."

Nobody spoke for five full seconds. Without highland repeaters and cross-basin clearing, river barges stopped trading grain for timber, coastal clinics ran out of mountain willow-bark painkillers, and machine parts stayed stranded fifty miles from broken pumps.

Beside Elara, Silas got slowly to his feet. He didn't step into the ring or raise his voice, just stood with his thumbs hooked in his belt looking straight at Thorne.

"Your own linemen helped us string that ridge cable through waist-deep snow four winters back, Thorne," Silas said evenly. "Cut that wire out of panic tonight, and half the people in this building won't trust your word next time an ice storm takes your transformers out."

Thorne's gaze flickered once toward Silas's singed jacket sleeve before hardening again. "My first oath is keeping highland children breathing till May, Silas."

For five more hours the argument ground back and forth across the chilling mill as daylight died behind the high windows and lantern smoke thickened under the rafters. Elara walked bench to bench until her voice went ragged, showing orchardists and river skippers exact bushel margins on her slate. Half listened; half stared past her shoulder toward the unseen glow on the southern sky.

Without a sheriff or an army, no council could compel a single signature.

When Elias finally struck the iron tire-rim at midnight to adjourn, the floor had split into jagged pieces:

Thorne's twelve Northern Ridge fellowships kept their passes shut and their surpluses frozen behind local-floor notices. Harken's three Iron Creek mining camps walked out the west doors twenty minutes early without shaking a single hand, muttering openly about filing permanent exit notices by morning. Out on the eastern bench, Brenner left promising Blackwood Siding's gates would stay padlocked regardless of what any review panel ruled. And the five Northern Plateau orchard delegates slipped quietly out to their trucks already tapping new reserve numbers into their slates.

Only the central creek valleys, the Riverine barge towns, and Priya's battered urban blocks agreed to staff and supply the highway way-stations directly, with Malik's Coastal Port crews promising covert night barges of tarps, fish oil, and hardware while keeping harbor gates shut by day.

Outside in the gravel yard, freezing wind drove dry ice pellets sideways across the parked flatbeds and cargo bikes. Silas unplugged their heavy charging lead from the mill post, coiled the stiff rubber cable into the truck bed without speaking, and climbed behind the wheel.

Inside the dark cab, their breath frosted the inside of the windshield before the defroster fan began to click. Silas rested both gloved hands at ten and two on the cracked plastic wheel without turning the key right away.

"Iron Creek's holding back our autumn alloy pack train if Harken files exit papers," Silas said quietly, staring into the black alders beyond the headlights. "And without Northern Ridge hydro backing us up, running heaters for fifty roadside tents is going to pull our creek batteries flat inside a week."

Elara leaned her head back against the cold vinyl headrest, eyes burning from stove smoke. Down the southern grade, a faint orange smudge still stained the underside of the clouds.

"Did we just promise warmth we can't generate, Silas?" she asked softly.

Silas switched the drive motor on; beneath the floorboards the inverter woke with a low, steady whine.

"Check the battery curve again on Thursday," he said, easing the flatbed out onto the rutted river road. "Tonight we string tent wire."
