\chapter{The Bring-Up of The Collective: From Simulation to Reality}

Chapter 9 described how a group of people can found a single Node. This chapter steps back to ask a larger question. How does The Collective as a whole come into being: the shared software, the culture of practice around it, and the first network of Nodes, before most people have ever seen a sensor or signed a Compact?

The answer this chapter proposes has three strands. The software is built in the open and tested against hard scenarios. A game, Oasis, is offered honestly to anyone who wants to play it, and it teaches through play what the Stack asks of people. And real Nodes arise when people, some of whom met through the game and many of whom did not, choose to found them. Like Chapter 9, this is a suggested approach. It describes a path that seems wise, not a schedule anyone must keep.

Earlier drafts told a different story, in which the game was a Trojan Horse, its players unwittingly funded the movement, their play was tracked and converted into equity and governance weight, and one day the game silently switched from simulated data to real valves. Every part of that story has been withdrawn. It was dishonest to the people it depended on, unlawful in most jurisdictions, unsafe, and contrary to the architecture of the preceding chapters. What remains is simpler and, we believe, stronger.

\section{Principles of Bring-Up}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{Honest invitation.} Everyone who plays, buys, or contributes knows what Oasis is and what it supports.
  \item \textit{Play for its own sake.} Oasis must be worth playing as a game, without manipulation.
  \item \textit{Simulation never acts.} Nothing done in the game moves anything in the real world.
  \item \textit{Play is not labour, and it is not power.} Game progress never converts into money, equity, credentials, or Reputational Wealth.
  \item \textit{Privacy by default.} Oasis collects as little as possible about its players, and nothing about where they live.
  \item \textit{Real competence is earned in reality.} The game prepares people; real-world permissions come through real apprenticeship.
  \item \textit{Open by design.} The Stack's software, the game's systems, and the lessons of every Node are a commons.
\end{itemize}

\section{Building the Stack and Oasis Through Scenarios}

The Collective begins in software. The Sovereign Stack is developed together with Oasis, a multiplayer simulation of communities, ecosystems, and infrastructure, using the Scenario Driven Development method described in Chapter 1.

\subsection{Two Kinds of Scenario}

Scenario Driven Development has two aims. The first is to model the local commons faithfully: the energy needs of a settlement, the water retention of particular soils, the yield of a food forest over decades, the health of the living systems a Node depends on. The second is to model \textit{legacy pressure}, because a Node is born into a world that does not always welcome it. Scenarios therefore include supply-chain disruptions, price shocks for imported parts, regulatory obstruction, the closing of bank accounts, and the escalating forms of hostility described in Chapter 9, from inspections to eviction operations. The Oasis simulation models these pressures across four regimes, from below the radar to active siege, so that both the software and its players can learn how a community responds well at each level.

Scenarios may draw on public, aggregate data, such as climate records, soil surveys, and published price indices, to keep the pressures realistic. They never draw on data about individual people. The goal is not to prove that the Stack is invulnerable, which no system is, but to find its weaknesses in simulation, where finding them costs nothing.

\subsection{The Oasis Game Design Document}

Development of Oasis is guided by a Game Design Document that avoids the usual shortcuts of commercial games. There are no arbitrary hit points or cosmetic progression trees. The game is a systems simulator: water flows in litres per second, energy is stored in kilowatt-hours, crop yields follow the balance of nutrients in the soil, and the economy runs on simulated exergy rather than money. If a simulated community overdraws its energy and water, its infrastructure degrades, its people struggle, and the settlement may fail.

The simulated people of Oasis have rich inner lives. Their moods, relationships, fatigue, and conflicts are modelled in detail, because a community simulator that ignored psychology would teach the wrong lessons. That modelling applies only to simulated agents. Oasis never builds a psychological profile of the person playing it.

The game's interface is built from the same components as the real interface of Layer 6, so that what players learn about reading a workflow, weighing a proposal, or casting a ballot carries over. Players sign simulated workflows with game keys, delegate simulated Reputational Wealth, and resolve simulated disputes. None of these keys, credits, or reputations exists outside the game.

\section{Releasing Oasis: An Honest Invitation}

When the simulation is stable, Oasis is released to the public. It is published by The Collective's cooperative legal entity, described in Chapter 8, and it says plainly what it is: a game about building resilient, self-governing communities, made by a cooperative that uses its income to develop open-source tools for real communities and to help them get started. Anyone who buys or plays the game knows this before they begin.

\subsection{Game Income as Residual Export}

Revenue from Oasis is export in the sense of Chapter 8, and the same rules apply. The cooperative raises fiat only against a ratified list of needs, such as development costs, servers for those who want them, and support for new Nodes. When income exceeds those needs, the surplus does not become a war chest. It lowers the price of the game, or makes it free, for the next cycle. A game that grew more expensive as it grew more popular would be optimising for revenue, and that is precisely what The Collective's economics forbid.

\subsection{No Dark Patterns}

Oasis is designed to be good to play, not compulsive. It has no loot boxes, no pay-to-win purchases, no streaks that punish rest, and no artificial urgency. Its appeal comes from the depth of its systems, the stories that emerge from them, the satisfaction of mastery, and the company of other players, the intrinsic rewards that keep people coming back to the games they love. Children may well play it, and the game treats their data with the strictest care the law requires, collecting as little as possible.

\subsection{Play Is Not Labour}

Scholars of games have long noted the ways the industry turns play into unpaid work, a phenomenon Julian Kücklich called ``playbour'' \parencite{kucklich2005}. Earlier drafts would have made Oasis an extreme case: tracking each player's contributions as labour and converting them into real-world equity and governance weight. That would have exploited players, invited compulsive grinding, and in many jurisdictions created an unregistered security, since an expectation of profit from an enterprise run by others is the core of the test set out in \textcite{howey1946}. Oasis does none of this. What players gain from playing is fun, skill, and understanding. If they choose to help the project in other ways, such as reporting bugs, proposing designs, or joining playtests, they do so knowingly, with credit and thanks, and without any claim to ownership or power.

\subsection{Place Without Tracking}

Players naturally want to play in a world that resembles their own. Earlier drafts proposed reading each player's real location and matching the simulation to it. That design is withdrawn. Collecting players' precise locations would violate the principles of purpose limitation and data minimisation found in modern privacy law, such as the GDPR \parencite{gdpr2016}. Worse, a central map of everyone interested in founding autonomous communities would be exactly the kind of list that the hostile actors described in Chapter 9 would most want to obtain.

Instead, players choose their setting themselves: an urban block, a rural valley, a coastal town, a dry plateau. If they want a closer match, they can load public geographic data for any region onto their own device, where it never leaves their control. Oasis does not know where its players live, and it keeps no registry of who is interested in what.

\section{From Players to Neighbours}

Oasis does not found Nodes. People do.

\subsection{How Real Nodes Begin}

Some players will want to try for real. The game can point them toward the beginning of Chapter 9: gathering with their neighbours, sharing meals, starting something small. If players want to find others nearby, they do so by their own choice and through means that do not centralise their identities, such as local meetups, community notice boards, or introductions through people they already know. Many Nodes will be founded by people who have never played the game at all, and that is as it should be.

\subsection{Support for New Nodes}

As the cooperative's income allows, and always against its ratified needs list, it can support new founding groups with equipment, training, and modest grants. Support is a gift, or a loan on clear terms, to a community, never an investment that yields a return or a stake. It is offered on transparent criteria, such as the readiness of a group's Compact, its relationships with its neighbours, and its need, and never on how well its members play the game. Groups with the fewest resources, who might otherwise never be able to begin, should come first.

\subsection{Oasis as a Shadow Simulator}

Once a real Node exists, Oasis serves it as a shadow simulator, under the rules of Chapters 3 and 7. A community can describe its actual watershed, buildings, and systems to Oasis and rehearse proposed workflows there before ratifying them. Oasis produces simulated telemetry, always tagged as simulated, so that it can stress-test the Stack without ever being mistaken for a real measurement.

The direction of that relationship never reverses. Earlier drafts described the game ``flipping'' to live telemetry, so that adjusting a valve in the game would open a real valve in the street. That will never happen. Real actuation happens only in reality mode, which is visually and audibly unmistakable. It requires a person to sign on their own device after the read-back of Chapter 7, and it passes through Layer 5's ratification, the Orchestrator's envelopes, the Ledger, and the devices of Layer 2. The game never holds real keys, and game mechanics never appear where the stakes are real.

\subsection{What Carries Over}

What a player carries from Oasis into a real Node is what anyone carries from practice: familiarity, understanding, and confidence. Nothing else crosses over. Game achievements do not become credentials, simulated reputation does not become Reputational Wealth, and hours played confer no standing. A new member who has played for a thousand hours and one who has never played begin with the same membership standing. Players' game histories remain private to them.

\section{Learning in Simulation, Qualifying in Reality}

How much does a game really teach? The evidence on serious games is encouraging but modest. Meta-analyses find that well-designed games can improve knowledge and some cognitive skills, but results vary widely, and the benefits for practical, physical competence are far less established \parencite{girard2013}. No simulation can teach the feel of a corroded fitting, the fear of a live circuit, or the judgement that comes from having been wrong in the field.

Oasis therefore prepares people; it does not qualify them. Before someone receives permission to work on high-voltage systems, pressurised water, or other dangerous infrastructure in a real Node, they learn through apprenticeship with experienced Stewards. Their competence is attested by the peers who watched them work, as Chapter 1 describes, and they hold a legal licence wherever the law requires one, obtained through real training as Chapter 8 sets out. A guild may choose to include particular Oasis exercises in its training, much as pilots train in simulators, but a simulation alone is never sufficient grounds for real-world authority.

\section{Oasis in a Mature Collective}

Earlier drafts imagined that Oasis would one day ``drop the facade'' and become the Semantic Interface of Layer 6. It had no facade to drop, and it does not become Layer 6. As Chapter 7 describes, Oasis and the real interface share components but remain distinct modes, and each Node adopts interface improvements discovered in the game only through its own Layer 5.

In a mature Collective, Oasis remains several things at once: a game people simply enjoy; a place where newcomers can learn how a Node works before joining one; a shadow simulator in which communities rehearse their decisions; and a laboratory in which the Stack's designers test new ideas against hard scenarios. Its purpose throughout is the one it had from the start: to let people practise a better way of living, safely and joyfully, until they are ready to build it.

\section{Protecting the Bring-Up}

The bring-up has its own vulnerabilities. The cooperative that publishes Oasis, the developers who build the Stack, and the community of players are all visible, and all could become targets of the pressures Chapter 9 describes.

Openness is the first protection. The Stack's code and the game's systems are published as open source, so there is nothing hidden to expose and nothing secret to seize. The cooperative complies carefully with consumer-protection, privacy, and securities law, because a legal misstep at this stage could end the project before any Node exists. No registry of players, Nodes, or members is kept anywhere, so there is no list to subpoena or steal. Disinformation is answered with transparency: published accounts, open meetings, and plain explanations of what The Collective is and is not. And the instruments of protection described in Chapter 9 apply to the people doing this work as much as to any founding group.

The bring-up of The Collective, then, is not a campaign of infiltration. It is an open invitation, extended through software anyone can read and a game anyone can play, to imagine a better way of living, practise it, and, if they choose, build it with their neighbours.
