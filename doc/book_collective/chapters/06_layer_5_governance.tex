\chapter{Layer 5: Governance and the Web of Trust}

Layer 5 is where the community decides. Layers 1 through 4 describe what is physically true, what is affordable, and how decisions are carried out; Layer 5 determines which decisions are legitimate, who may take part in making them, and how they may later be changed.

Layer 5 interfaces only with Layer 4 below it and Layer 6 above it. It sets policy and confers legitimacy; it never executes. It does not read the Thermodynamic Ledger, it does not touch hardware, and it does not render language for people. Physical facts reach it only as reports from the Orchestrator, and human intent reaches it only through the Semantic Interface.

Layer 5 is also the only meta-recursive layer in the Stack. It governs at three levels at once:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the layer below it, by ratifying, migrating, and draining Layer 4 workflows and by setting the standing policies the Orchestrator enforces;
  \item its own domain: identity, Reputational Wealth, delegation, councils, membership, and sanctions;
  \item itself: the rules by which governance rules are made, amended, and challenged.
\end{itemize}
A defect at the third level can make every other defect permanent, which is why this chapter treats self-governance with particular care.

The original premise of Layer 5 is that work sustains the community, and that those who do the work should guide its evolution. That premise is retained, with a necessary companion: those who are \textit{affected} by a decision must have a voice in it, whether or not they performed the work. Work earns stewardship over \textit{how} things are done; being affected earns a voice in \textit{what} is done and \textit{whether}. The two-chamber structure in Section~\ref{sec:l5-levels} gives each principle its own institution.

The chapter proceeds as follows: Layer 5's position in the Stack; identity; standing and Reputational Wealth; the three levels of governance; voting and tallying; culture, memory, and the collective dimension of governance; and finally the concrete data structures.

\subsection{What Layer 5 Does Not Do}

Layer 5 does not enforce anything itself. Enforcement of rules about physical action is performed by the Orchestrator against the Ledger; enforcement of rules about people is performed by people, through the procedures in Section~\ref{sec:l5-sanctions}. Layer 5 does not parse or produce natural language; deliberation, explanation, and testimony are Layer 6 activities whose results reach Layer 5 as structured, signed records. And Layer 5 holds no legacy legal identity. Names, registrations, and legal persons belong to Layer 7, and collisions between community rules and legacy law are surfaced upward through Layer 6 to Layer 7 rather than handled here.

\section{Identity: The Web of Trust}

\subsection{Identity without the State}

In the legacy world, identity is issued by a state or a corporation, which can revoke it, freeze the accounts attached to it, or deny access to infrastructure. Layer 5 replaces issued identity with a \textit{Web of Trust}: a Steward's identity is a set of cryptographic keys and the peer attestations bound to them, not a row in someone else's database.

\subsection{Sybil Resistance and Physical Seeding}

A governance system that counts people must be able to tell one person from many. Identities in Layer 5 are seeded physically. A new identity becomes a member only when established members co-sign it in person, extending the physical web of trust described in Chapter 4. The number of required co-signers and their independence from one another are meta-rules (Section~\ref{sec:l5-tiers}). Uniqueness matters most in the commons chamber, where each member has exactly one vote; a Sybil identity there is a forged ballot.

\subsection{Pairwise Identifiers and Selective Disclosure}

Earlier drafts of this architecture argued that splitting attestations across council-specific storage would make surveillance infeasible. It does not: if the same key appears in the Water Council's records and the Energy Council's records, anyone can join them. Privacy has to be built into the identifiers themselves.

Each Steward therefore holds a master identity that is never presented to councils. For each council they join, they derive a separate \textit{pairwise identifier} that cannot be linked to their other identifiers without their cooperation. Attestations are issued as Verifiable Credentials \parencite{w3c2022vc} using signature schemes that support selective disclosure. When eligibility must be shown, for example certification for high-voltage work, the Steward presents a zero-knowledge proof that they hold a valid credential of the required type, revealing neither the credential's other contents nor their identity in other councils.

Credentials are verified in Layer 5. The Orchestrator never parses credentials and never learns who performed a task; it receives only the answer that an eligible party has accepted it (Section~\ref{sec:l5-matching}).

\subsection{Key Loss, Recovery, and Revocation}

Losing a key must not mean losing one's place in the community. Each Steward splits a recovery secret among trusted people of their choosing, with any $M$ of $N$ shares sufficient to restore it \parencite{shamir1979}. Recovery re-binds the Steward's standing, attestations, and Reputational Wealth to a new key after the recovering parties confirm the request in person.

Credentials can be revoked: a certification can lapse, an issuer can discover an error, or a credential can be withdrawn as a sanction. Revocation is published through privacy-preserving revocation registries, so that verifiers can confirm a credential is still valid without learning which credential they are checking. Revocation used as a sanction is subject to due process (Section~\ref{sec:l5-dueprocess}).

\subsection{Storage and Computation}

Layer 5 maintains its own governance store, partitioned by council namespace and replicated across the Node's edge hardware. It is separate from the Thermodynamic Ledger. Councils, rules, rationale records, encrypted delegations, and ballots live here; exergy, telemetry, and actuation envelopes live in Layer 3. The two never share records, and Layer 5 never writes to the Ledger.

\section{Standing, Stewardship, and Reputational Wealth}

\subsection{Two Kinds of Standing}

Layer 5 distinguishes between two things a person can hold.

\textit{Membership standing} is held equally by every member, cannot be earned or lost through activity, and does not decay. It carries a vote in the commons chamber, the protections of the entrenched core (Section~\ref{sec:l5-tiers}), and an unconditional claim on the survival baseline defined in Chapter 5. It can be affected only through the due process described in Section~\ref{sec:l5-dueprocess}, and never so as to withdraw the baseline.

\textit{Reputational Wealth} is domain-specific weight in the stewardship chamber of a particular council. It records value delivered to the community in that domain. It is earned, it decays, and it is capped.

Separating the two ensures that a person who cannot work because of illness, disability, age, or caregiving loses influence over technical decisions in a given domain but never loses their voice, their rights, or their means of survival.

\subsection{How Reputational Wealth Is Earned}

When a task is completed, the Orchestrator sends a completion attestation upward, keyed to an anonymous claim handle (Section~\ref{sec:l5-matching}). Layer 5, which alone can link that handle to a pairwise identifier, records Reputational Wealth for that Steward in the relevant council. People do not mint exergy; the physical result of their work enters the Ledger through Proof-of-Thermodynamic-Work, as Chapter 4 describes, and accrues to the commons.

Work that the Orchestrator never sees also counts. Caregiving, teaching, mediation, and cultural work are attested by the councils responsible for those domains, so that Reputational Wealth does not systematically reward machine-visible labor over human care.

Reputational Wealth is not a token. It cannot be transferred, sold, pooled, or exported to govern another Node. Its effects may radiate outward through the Web of Trust as a signal of reliability, but its weight applies only where it was earned.

\subsection{Decay, Caps, and Care Leave}

Without decay, early contributors would accumulate permanent weight and the system would harden into a gerontocracy. With naive decay, it would turn into a treadmill that punishes rest. Layer 5 combines three mechanisms to avoid both failures.

\textit{Decay} is a weighting function applied at tally time, not a deletion. Each contribution's weight follows a half-life measured from the time the work was completed. The half-life is set per council as a meta-rule and is typically measured in years rather than months.

\textit{Caps} limit concentration. Weight grows sub-linearly with contribution, for example logarithmically, so that ten times the work does not yield ten times the voice. No individual's weight, including delegated weight, may exceed a fixed share of a chamber's total.

\textit{Care leave} pauses decay. A Steward may declare leave for illness, burnout, parenthood, bereavement, or any reason they choose not to state. The declaration is private: it is made through Layer 6, recorded against the Steward's pairwise identifiers, and never published. While leave is active, the decay clock stops.

\subsection{Liquid Delegation with Limits}

No one can follow every decision in every council. A Steward may delegate their stewardship weight in a council to someone they trust, and revoke the delegation at any time. Unbounded delegation, however, reliably concentrates power, and citizens rarely monitor delegates closely enough to revoke in time \parencite{michels1911}. Delegation in Layer 5 is therefore bounded.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Delegated weight counts toward the holder's cap.
  \item Delegated weight cannot be re-delegated. Chains are limited to one hop.
  \item Delegations expire after a period set as a meta-rule and must be actively renewed.
  \item A delegate's votes cast with delegated weight are public, because they are exercises of entrusted power. Who delegated to whom remains private.
\end{itemize}
Membership standing in the commons chamber cannot be delegated at all.

\section{The Three Levels of Governance}
\label{sec:l5-levels}

\subsection{Level One: Governing the Orchestrator}

\subsubsection{The Policy Lifecycle}

A change to the logic that runs the Node passes through the following steps.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A proposal is raised. It may start as plain-language intent through Layer 6; drawing BPMN is not a precondition for participation. Stewards with the relevant skills, assisted by Layer 6 tooling, translate the intent into a draft workflow.
  \item Workflows that affect physical reality are simulated in Oasis against historical and stress conditions. Domains under the Protocol of Refusal (Section~\ref{sec:l5-refusal}) are excluded from simulation as a decision input.
  \item The proposal is deliberated in the responsible council, with hearings for affected parties, including non-members where Section~\ref{sec:l5-neighbours} requires.
  \item The council decides using its ratified decision procedure and the chamber or chambers the decision requires.
  \item A rationale record is bound to the decision (Section~\ref{sec:l5-memory}).
  \item The ratified workflow is handed to Layer 4, whose compiler applies its safety checks. A workflow that fails them returns to the council and is not deployed.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{In-Flight Workflows}

When a council changes a workflow, running instances continue under the version on which they started, unless the council also ratifies a migration that maps old states to new ones, or orders a drain that sends every running instance to its compensation path. Changes to governance rules themselves (Level Three) never alter running workflows directly. They take effect through the ordinary policies they cause councils to revise.

\subsubsection{Escalations and Alerts}

The Orchestrator escalates decisions it is not permitted to make, chiefly conflicts between two inviolable floors. For each class of escalation, a meta-rule designates who decides, by which procedure, and within what time window. If no decision arrives within the window, the Orchestrator's safest available envelope remains in force. Alerts arriving from Layer 4 are routed by an audience policy that specifies who must be told for each alert class, for example the Stewards of an affected array or all residents of a flooded zone, before they are passed to Layer 6 for presentation.

\subsubsection{Task Matching}
\label{sec:l5-matching}

Task intents arrive from Layer 4 without any reference to people. Layer 5 determines eligibility from the required credentials, using the zero-knowledge proofs described above, and passes offers to Layer 6. When an eligible Steward accepts, Layer 5 returns an anonymous claim handle to the Orchestrator, which reserves resources against it. On completion, the Orchestrator's attestation, keyed to the same handle, allows Layer 5 to record Reputational Wealth. Refusal is always free, and no record of it is kept against the Steward.

Self-reported capacity follows the same direction of travel. Stewards report it through Layer 6, Layer 5 aggregates it without exposing individuals, and only the resulting community ceiling is passed down to Layer 4.

\subsection{Level Two: Governing Its Own Domain}

\subsubsection{Boundaries and Membership}

Every physically based council declares who belongs to it, normally those who live within or draw from the resource it governs, and how people join and leave. Gravitating toward a council is not enough: clearly defined boundaries are the first condition for a commons that endures \parencite{ostrom1990}. Affection-based councils set their own membership rules but, as described below, cannot bind any physical commons.

\subsubsection{Two Chambers}

Each physically based council has two chambers.

The \textit{stewardship chamber} weights members by Reputational Wealth in that domain. It decides questions of method: how a ratified goal is implemented, which workflow design is sound, and which technical trade-offs to accept.

The \textit{commons chamber} gives one vote to every member affected by the council's resource, regardless of Reputational Wealth. It decides questions of purpose: what the resource is for, how it is shared, what limits apply, and whether a project should proceed.

Ordinary operational changes need only the stewardship chamber, unless a set number of commons members call them up for review. Allocation rules, limits, and new projects need both chambers. Core and meta rules always need both. This arrangement satisfies the principle that those affected by operational rules can take part in modifying them \parencite{ostrom1990}, while preserving the competence of those who do the work.

\subsubsection{Pluggable Decision Procedures}

Communities reach agreement in different ways, and one protocol must not impose one culture. Each council selects its decision procedure from a set the Stack implements: weighted or equal voting with defined thresholds, consent-based decision-making in which proposals pass unless a member raises a reasoned objection, unanimity for small groups, and deliberative panels drawn by lot. Research on face-to-face consensus shows that unitary and adversarial modes of democracy suit different conditions \parencite{mansbridge1980}; the Stack supports both rather than choosing for the community. A council's choice of procedure is a meta-rule, changeable only at the meta tier's threshold.

\subsubsection{Nesting and Subsidiarity}

Councils nest by geography: a Node council within a zone council within a bioregional council. Decisions are taken at the smallest level that contains everyone affected. An encompassing council sets constraints, such as ecological floors on a shared watershed, and nested councils customize freely within them. When a nested council's rule conflicts with an encompassing council's constraint, the constraint prevails, and the dispute goes to the encompassing council's review process rather than to the Orchestrator.

Affection-based councils sit outside this geography. A craft guild, a global standards body reconstituted as a Layer 5 council (the IEEE or the ACM, say), a sports federation, a children's play arrangement, or a fan club governs its own namespace, resources, and credentials. It cannot bind any physical commons, and its credentials carry weight in a physical council only if that council chooses to recognize them.

\subsubsection{Enforcement, Sanctions, and Restoration}
\label{sec:l5-sanctions}

Earlier drafts claimed that cybernetic enforcement made coercion obsolete. That claim is withdrawn. The Orchestrator can refuse to open a valve that a quota forbids, but it cannot stop someone with a wrench, and an algorithm that cut a person off from resources would itself be coercion. Layer 5 therefore separates two kinds of enforcement.

\textit{Constraints on actions} are enforced automatically. The Orchestrator refuses envelopes that breach floors or quotas, whoever requested them. These constraints never single out a person, and they never touch the survival baseline.

\textit{Responses to people} who break rules, including physical bypasses that the Orchestrator cannot prevent, are handled by people. The first response is always restorative: a facilitated process that brings together those harmed and the person responsible to understand what happened and agree on repair \parencite{zehr1990}. Only if restoration fails or is refused do graduated sanctions apply, escalating in proportion to the seriousness and repetition of the breach, as enduring commons require \parencite{ostrom1990}. Sanctions may include temporary suspension of particular credentials, reduced stewardship weight in the affected domain, or suspension of non-baseline privileges. Every sanction is time-limited and carries a defined path back. No sanction may withdraw the survival baseline. Withdrawing membership standing is a core-tier decision with the full protections of due process.

\subsubsection{Due Process and Appeals}
\label{sec:l5-dueprocess}

Any sanction against a person, including revocation of a credential, requires notice of the alleged breach, an opportunity to respond, the right to be accompanied by a person of one's choosing, a decision with reasons recorded, and a right of appeal.

Appeals, and challenges to council decisions on the ground that they violate the entrenched core, are heard by a Review Council whose members are drawn by lot from the membership and serve short, staggered terms. Selection by lot keeps review independent of the factions that won the original decision. When a challenge shows a plausible violation of the core, the Review Council may suspend enforcement of an ordinary rule pending decision; the suspension reaches Layer 4 as a policy change. A challenge can never suspend an ecological floor or the survival baseline in a way that permits harm, because those protections exist precisely to hold during disputes.

\subsection{Level Three: Governing Itself}

\subsubsection{The Rule of Recognition and the Genesis Compact}

Every legal order needs an ultimate rule that identifies which other rules are valid \parencite{hart1961}. In Layer 5, that rule is the \textit{Genesis Compact}: the founding agreement ratified unanimously, in person, by the founding Stewards who form the Genesis Council during the Genesis Protocol (Chapter 9). The Compact states the entrenched core, the meta-rules, and the procedure by which each may be amended. A rule is valid in the Node if and only if it was enacted by a procedure the Compact, as amended by its own procedures, recognizes.

Unanimity at founding solves the bootstrapping problem: no one is bound by a constitution they did not accept. People who join later accept the Compact as a condition of membership, and their right of exit (below) keeps that acceptance meaningful.

\subsubsection{Amendment Safeguards}

Three safeguards address the self-amendment paradox, in which a rule could be used to weaken the rules that govern its own change. First, a threshold can be changed only by the threshold it currently requires, so a simple majority can never lower a supermajority requirement. Second, the clauses that entrench the core are themselves part of the core. Third, every rule above the ordinary tier carries a review date. At that date its rationale record is revisited and the rule is either reaffirmed or put up for amendment, so that entrenched rules remain understood rather than merely obeyed.

\subsubsection{Emergency Powers}

Emergency playbooks are ratified in advance and executed by the Orchestrator when their triggers appear. Layer 5 bounds them in three ways. Each playbook has a maximum duration after which it lapses unless the responsible council votes to extend it. No playbook may override the entrenched core. And every activation is reviewed by the council once the emergency ends, with the review recorded alongside the playbook's rationale.

\subsubsection{Exit}

Every member may leave, taking their personal records and credentials with them. A community that can be left must persuade rather than compel, and the availability of exit disciplines the use of voice \parencite{hirschman1970}. Questions about dividing shared physical assets when a group of members wishes to form a separate Node are resolved by the encompassing council under the bioregion's rules.

\section{Voting and Tallying}

\subsection{Secret, Verifiable Ballots}

Public, signed votes would make every ballot visible to family, neighbors, and anyone seeking to buy or coerce votes. Layer 5 instead uses end-to-end verifiable voting. Each member can confirm that their own encrypted ballot was included, and anyone can confirm that the published tally correctly combines all included ballots, without any individual ballot being revealed \parencite{adida2008helios}. Ballots are tallied homomorphically, under encryption. A voter proves eligibility and, in the stewardship chamber, their weight with a zero-knowledge proof rather than by revealing their identity. To resist coercion, members may re-cast their ballot privately before the close, and only the last valid ballot counts, so a coerced vote can be silently replaced \parencite{juels2005coercion}.

\subsection{The Limits of the Mathematics}

A tally can be computed exactly; legitimacy cannot. A count proves only that a procedure was followed. Whether the procedure deserves allegiance depends on whether people were heard, whether the reasons were understood, and whether the decision fits who the community takes itself to be. The final section deals with these questions.

\section{Culture, Memory, and the Collective}

The individual protections above, such as care leave, restorative process, private ballots, and free refusal, address people as persons. Governance also has a collective dimension, made of shared meaning, memory, obligation across generations, and the things a community will not reduce to numbers. Layer 5 gives each of these a concrete mechanism.

\subsection{Legitimacy Beyond Procedure}

Some decisions should be taken in person, not only on a screen. Ratifying the Genesis Compact, amending the core, and admitting new members by physical co-signature are performed in assemblies, at times and in forms each community chooses. The Stack records the outcome; the assembly is where the community recognizes itself in it.

\subsection{Collective Memory: Rationale Records}
\label{sec:l5-memory}

A community that forgets why it made a rule will eventually remove a fence whose purpose it no longer remembers \parencite{chesterton1929}. Every rule ratified in Layer 5 is therefore bound by hash to a \textit{rationale record}. The record states the problem addressed, the evidence considered, the alternatives rejected and why, the dissents raised, and the conditions under which the rule should be revisited. Rationale records are authored by people through Layer 6 and stored in Layer 5 as signed payloads. A proposal to repeal or amend a rule must respond to its rationale record, and the response becomes part of the record.

\subsection{Intergenerational Duty}

Some decisions outlast everyone who makes them: an aquifer drawn down, a food forest planted, a valley flooded. Any proposal whose effects are irreversible, or extend beyond a horizon set as a meta-rule (on the order of decades), is tagged as long-horizon. Long-horizon proposals must be reviewed by a Future Generations panel, drawn by lot and mandated to argue for those not yet born. The panel holds a suspensive veto: it can delay a decision and require reconsideration with its objections answered in the rationale record, but it cannot block indefinitely. Comparable institutions exist in legacy law, for example the statutory duty to consider long-term impacts and the office of a Future Generations Commissioner \parencite{wales2015future}, and the principle echoes the widely cited ethic of considering the seventh generation.

\subsection{The Protocol of Refusal}
\label{sec:l5-refusal}

A community may decide that some places, practices, or relationships are not to be measured, simulated, or optimized: a burial ground, a grove, a ceremony, the private life of a household. The \textit{Protocol of Refusal} lets the commons chamber designate such a domain. A designation has the following effects:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the domain cannot appear as an optimization target in any workflow;
  \item it is excluded from Oasis simulation as an input to decisions;
  \item no sensors are installed in it beyond those the designation explicitly permits;
  \item Layer 4 receives it as a floor, so no actuation envelope may affect it except to protect it.
\end{itemize}
Designation is deliberately asymmetric. Creating one requires the commons chamber's ordinary threshold; removing one requires the meta tier's threshold and a rationale record. It should be easier to protect something from quantification than to expose it.

\subsection{Cultural Plurality}

Nodes are not required to share a culture. Beyond the entrenched core and the interfaces between layers, each Node's Genesis Compact, decision procedures, assemblies, and rituals are its own. Interoperation between Nodes depends only on the core and the interfaces, not on any shared way of reaching agreement.

\subsection{Kinship and the Right to Informality}

Councils coordinate; they are not kinship. A Public Health Council can organize care for elders and mediation for disputes, but it does not replace families, friendships, or communities of affection. Any group may exist without a council namespace, without credentials, and without recording anything in Layer 5. The governance protocol is available to relationships that want it and imposes nothing on those that do not.

\subsection{Non-Stewards and Neighbouring Communities}
\label{sec:l5-neighbours}

Consistent with the commitments of Chapter 1, people outside the community are neither adversaries nor background. Non-members affected by a decision, such as a neighbouring town downstream on a shared river or a non-member household within a Node's boundary, have three channels. They may petition to place an item on a council's agenda. They may give testimony at hearings, which councils must hold before deciding matters that affect them. And they are owed a written response that addresses their testimony and is attached to the decision's rationale record. Their participation is recorded without requiring them to adopt any identity in the Web of Trust. Where a decision collides with the legal rights of legacy neighbours, the matter is surfaced through Layer 6 to Layer 7.
