\chapter{Layer 6: The Semantic Layer}

Layer 6 is where people meet the Stack. It translates between human expression and the structured records of Layer 5; it hosts deliberation; and it carries the community's decisions outward to Layer 7, the layer that faces the legacy world.

Layer 6 interfaces only with Layer 5 below it and Layer 7 above it. It does not read the Thermodynamic Ledger, it does not communicate with the Orchestrator, and it has no knowledge of hardware. Everything it knows about the physical world arrives as structured records from Layer 5, which in turn received them from Layer 4.

Layer 6 is also the only layer in which a machine, a local language model, stands between people and their own governance. A mistranslated proposal can become law, a biased summary can steer a debate, and an injected instruction can impersonate a decision. The chapter is therefore organized around a single rule: \textit{the machine assists, people author, and only people sign}.

Earlier drafts described Layer 6 as the interface that would one day be ``the only interface humanity needs to interact with the world.'' That claim is withdrawn. Layer 6 is the community's interface to its own infrastructure and governance. The land, assemblies, craft, and unmediated human company remain the primary ways people meet the world; Layer 6 serves them and must never replace them. As legacy systems recede, Layer 7 will shrink, but Layer 6 will still be one tool among many, not a total environment.

\section{Design Principles}

The following principles apply throughout the chapter.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{Human authorship.} Any record entering Layer 5 is authored and signed by a person. The machine may draft; it never signs, and it never submits on anyone's behalf.
  \item \textit{Read-back before signing.} A person signs only after seeing a plain-language rendering generated deterministically from the exact structured payload they are about to sign.
  \item \textit{Grounded or silent.} Generated explanations must cite the Layer 5 records they rely on. A claim with no source is not shown.
  \item \textit{Deterministic where safety matters.} Safety-critical alerts use fixed, tested wording, never generated text.
  \item \textit{Graceful degradation.} Every function remains usable without a language model.
  \item \textit{Privacy by construction.} Layer 6 renders each person's own records to that person and public aggregates to everyone. It never assembles a map of who trusts, delegates to, or votes for whom.
  \item \textit{Plurality.} Vocabularies, interfaces, and deliberative formats can be adapted to each Node's languages and culture.
  \item \textit{The right to unmediated space.} Any conversation can be held without machine processing.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What Layer 6 Does Not Do}

Layer 6 does not read telemetry or device identifiers, does not explain the Orchestrator by inspecting it, and does not tally votes or store identity. A person's keys live on a device that person controls, and signing happens there. Layer 6 provides the interface to that device but never holds the keys. When Layer 6 explains why an irrigation workflow changed, it is rendering an alert and rationale that Layer 5 passed upward, not inspecting Layer 4.

\subsection{The Semantic Model and Its Provenance}

To explain events in human terms, Layer 6 maintains a semantic model linking concepts such as ``Greenhouse B,'' ``winter food supply,'' and ``Pump 3,'' and the relationships between them, for example that Greenhouse B supplies a fifth of the community's winter vegetables. Every fact in this model arrives as a structured description from Layer 5. The model contains no telemetry, no device addresses, and nothing Layer 5 did not provide.

The model is expressed in JSON-LD \parencite{w3c2020jsonld}, which lets each Node publish its own vocabulary while mapping it to a small shared core needed for interoperation. Heavyweight ontology reasoning is avoided on edge hardware. More importantly, a single comprehensive ontology is avoided on principle: whoever defines the categories shapes what can be said. Layer 6 therefore supports several overlapping graphs, so that a river can be described at the same time as an irrigation source in one vocabulary and as a sacred being in another, without either description overriding the other. Vocabularies are governed objects. They are adopted and amended through Layer 5, each with a rationale record.

\section{Translation}

\subsection{From People to Layer 5: Draft, Read-Back, Sign}

Every record a person sends to Layer 5 follows the same sequence.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The person expresses intent in any supported language or modality: typed text, speech, a paper form transcribed by a helper, or a structured form.
  \item A drafting model converts the intent into a structured payload of the type Layer 5 expects, such as a proposal, delegation, appeal, or self-report. For proposals that change workflows, Stewards with the relevant skills may refine the resulting draft BPMN, as Chapter 6 describes; drawing BPMN is never required to take part.
  \item If the intent is ambiguous, the drafting model stops and asks. It does not guess. Unresolved ambiguity is referred to a human interpreter.
  \item The payload is rendered back to the person in plain language by a deterministic renderer: fixed templates driven by the payload's fields, not a language model. The read-back therefore shows what will actually be signed, not what the model believes it wrote.
  \item The person confirms, edits, or abandons. Confirmation is a signature made on the person's own device.
  \item The signed payload is sent to Layer 5 with a provenance tag (Section~\ref{sec:l6-provenance}).
\end{enumerate}
The deterministic read-back is the most important control in the layer. It defends against model error, mistranslation, and injected instructions alike, because nothing reaches Layer 5 that the signer did not see rendered from the payload itself.

\subsection{Provenance of Text}
\label{sec:l6-provenance}

Every piece of text in Layer 6 carries one of four provenance tags, and the interface displays each distinctly:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{human\_authored}: written by a person and signed by them;
  \item \texttt{machine\_drafted\_human\_approved}: drafted by a model, read back, and signed by a person;
  \item \texttt{machine\_generated}: produced by a model and not approved by anyone, such as explanations and summaries;
  \item \texttt{system\_authenticated}: issued by Layer 5 and signed, such as alerts, decisions, and tallies.
\end{itemize}
System-authenticated content is cryptographically signed, so a participant cannot impersonate a council or the system in a conversation.

\subsection{Languages, Dialects, and Oral Tradition}

Language models are strongest in the languages and registers most common in their training data and weakest in minority languages, dialects, and oral forms. Layer 6 compensates in four ways. Each Node maintains a controlled vocabulary mapping its own terms, including dialect and idiom, to the shared core. Communities may fine-tune their models on local corpora such as recorded speech, local writing, and oral histories, with the consent of those whose words are used. Spoken contributions are transcribed and shown back for correction before they enter any record. And human interpreters are a first-class part of the system, not a fallback of last resort.

\subsection{When Translation Fails or Is Disputed}

Any member may dispute a machine rendering, whether a summary that misstates a position or an explanation that misreads a decision. A disputed rendering is flagged wherever it appears, and the structured Layer 5 record always prevails over any rendering of it. Disputes and failures feed the model evaluations described in Section~\ref{sec:l6-models}.

\section{The Language Models}
\label{sec:l6-models}

\subsection{Running on Salvaged Hardware}

Layer 6 runs small language models compressed to fit the memory of salvaged hardware; post-training quantization reduces memory requirements severalfold at modest cost in quality \parencite{frantar2023gptq}. A typical arrangement places a small model on ordinary community machines for drafting and transcription, and a larger model on the best-provisioned server for grounded explanation. Responses take seconds rather than milliseconds, and the interface is designed around that.

\subsection{Inference as an Exergy Expenditure}

Every inference consumes electricity. Layer 6 operates within a standing computational allocation that is ratified in Layer 5 and accounted for by Layer 4 like any other computation (Chapter 5). When exergy is scarce, the allocation shrinks and Layer 6 degrades in a fixed order. Optional summaries go first, then elaboration of routine notices. Drafting assistance, deterministic read-back, and safety-critical alerts are always preserved, and the last two never depend on a language model.

\subsection{Graceful Degradation}

With no language model available, every Layer 6 function still works. Structured forms replace free-text drafting, templates replace explanations, raw records remain readable, and human interpreters and paper remain available. A community that loses its models loses convenience, not governance.

\subsection{Model Governance and Supply Chain}

Model weights, system prompts, retrieval corpora, and vocabularies are governed assets. Adopting or updating any of them is a Layer 5 decision with a rationale record. Every adopted asset is identified by a cryptographic hash, and Layer 6 refuses to load anything whose hash does not match a ratified record.

Before adoption, a candidate model is evaluated in public. The evaluation covers translation fidelity on the community's own test set, behavior in its minority languages, a bias audit, and red-team results. Models trained on legacy corpora carry the assumptions of those corpora: their dominant languages, their commercial framing, and their blind spots \parencite{bender2021parrots,noble2018}. Air-gapping a model does not remove what it learned. A corpus provenance registry records what each model was trained and fine-tuned on, and the interface indicates which model produced each generated text.

\section{Security}

\subsection{The Air-Gap Claim, Corrected}

Layer 6's models are not connected to corporate services, but Layer 6 is not isolated from adversarial input. It reads text from people and, through Layer 7, from the legacy world: invoices, contracts, correspondence, and legal notices. Text that a model reads can contain instructions the model may follow, a vulnerability known as indirect prompt injection \parencite{greshake2023injection}. It ranks first among the risks catalogued for language-model applications \parencite{owasp2023llm}.

\subsection{Separation of Reading and Drafting}

Layer 6 therefore separates the model that reads untrusted text from the model that drafts payloads.

The \textit{intake model} processes legacy documents and long deliberation threads. Its output is restricted to fixed schema fields, such as amounts, dates, parties, and the positions raised, and is tagged as derived from untrusted input. It cannot draft payloads.

The \textit{drafting model} receives only the person's own expressed intent and structured fields. It never sees raw untrusted text.

Neither model holds keys, and neither can submit anything. Signing happens only on a person's device after the deterministic read-back, which is the final defense: an injected instruction that somehow altered a draft would appear in the read-back for the signer to see.

\subsection{Private Data and Retrieval}

Records that Chapter 6 makes private are never embedded, indexed, or supplied to any model. These include ballots, delegation edges, care leave, self-reports, task refusals, and the links between a person's pairwise identifiers. Retrieval is scoped to the requesting person and to the council in which they are acting. Conversation logs are end-to-end encrypted, retained according to policy ratified in Layer 5, and never used for training without the explicit consent of every participant.

\subsection{Red-Teaming}

Translation fidelity and security are tested continuously. Adversarial exercises, including injection attempts embedded in simulated legacy documents in Oasis, are run against each adopted model, and the results are reported to Layer 5 as part of the model's record.

\section{Attention and Access}

\subsection{Calm Technology}

Layer 6 follows the principle that technology should inform without demanding attention, moving between the periphery and the center of awareness only as needed \parencite{weiser1996calm}. It does not measure engagement and does not optimize for time spent. It uses no streaks, badges, variable rewards, or infinite feeds, and it never shows reputation as a leaderboard.

\subsection{Participation Without a Screen}

Every function of Layer 6 is available without a screen. Proposals and decisions can be heard by voice and printed on paper. Paper ballots are cast and counted at assemblies under procedures that preserve secrecy and allow verification. Testimony can be given in person and transcribed for confirmation. Members may designate a trusted helper to assist them; a helper may read, explain, and transcribe but cannot sign, and assisted voting follows procedures that protect ballot secrecy. Sign-language interpretation is provided at assemblies and hearings.

\subsection{Accessibility}

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines \parencite{w3c2023wcag} are the minimum standard. Beyond them:
\begin{itemize}
  \item every BPMN diagram has an equivalent linear text description;
  \item every proposal has a plain-language version;
  \item layouts are predictable, and nothing other than emergencies is time-pressured;
  \item pacing, density, and modality can be adjusted to the person rather than the person to the interface.
\end{itemize}

\section{The Agora}

The Agora is the set of deliberative spaces Layer 6 hosts. Which formats a council uses follows from the decision procedure it has ratified in Layer 5 (Chapter 6).

\subsection{Deliberative Formats}

Layer 6 supports open forums for proposals and discussion, and structured argument maps that set claims, evidence, and objections side by side. It hosts deliberative panels drawn by lot, which hear evidence and reason together on behalf of the wider membership \parencite{fishkin1991}, as well as the Future Generations panels and Review Council hearings defined in Chapter 6. It provides hearings for non-members, and support for in-person assemblies: agendas, live captioning and translation, and records.

\subsection{The Machine's Role in Deliberation}

In deliberation, the machine may transcribe, translate, retrieve evidence with citations, help map arguments, and produce summaries. It is bound by four constraints:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Summaries are structured, with majority and minority positions in separate fields, each traceable to the contributions it summarizes.
  \item A summary proposed for a rationale record must be approved by representatives of each position it describes. Dissenters approve the description of their dissent.
  \item The machine never ranks options, recommends outcomes, or produces persuasive text on its own initiative.
  \item All generated content carries the \texttt{machine\_generated} tag.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Unmediated Spaces and the Protocol of Refusal}

Any member can read the raw transcript behind any summary. Any thread, channel, or meeting can be marked unmediated. In an unmediated space no machine transcribes, summarizes, embeds, links, or retains anything beyond what the participants themselves choose to record. Councils may also place conversations under the Protocol of Refusal defined in Chapter 6, extending it from places and practices to speech.

\subsection{Rendering Governance Without Exposing People}

Layer 6 shows each person their own standing, Reputational Wealth, delegations, and ballot receipts. It shows everyone the public aggregates listed in Chapter 6: proposals, rationale records, tallies, and votes cast with delegated weight. It does not show who holds reputation in a council as a ranked list, and it never displays who trusts, delegates to, or votes for whom.

\section{Non-Members and Neighbours}

Consistent with Chapter 1, people outside the community are neither adversaries nor background. Layer 6 provides non-members with a portal that requires no identity in the Web of Trust. It offers plain-language and voice interaction, the right to petition a council, the right to give testimony at hearings, and delivery of the written responses Chapter 6 requires. Visitors are not profiled and nothing about them is retained beyond what they choose to submit.

\section{Directing Layer 7}

The Node exists within the legacy world and must sometimes act in it. Suppose the community decides, through Layer 5, to buy medical equipment from a legacy supplier. Exergy Credits are worthless to that supplier, so fiat currency must be raised, typically through the Node's cooperative legal entity selling a service in the legacy market, and only against a ratified needs list and within the export ceiling described in Chapter 8. Layer 6 renders the ratified decision into instructions for Layer 7, which carries them out through its legal and financial instruments. Correspondence, invoices, and contracts returning from the legacy world arrive through Layer 7 and enter Layer 6 only via the intake pipeline described above. Where a Layer 5 decision collides with legacy law, the collision is surfaced through Layer 6 to Layer 7. Layer 6 never exposes the community's internal records to the legacy world.

\section{Oasis: A Shared Interface, Distinct Modes}

The interface players use to govern simulated bioregions in Oasis is built from the same components as the interface Stewards use in the real Node. Skills learned in the game therefore transfer to real governance, and players' difficulties reveal problems in the real interface before they cause harm. Sharing components does not mean sharing modes, and the following rules keep the two apart:
\begin{itemize}
  \item reality mode is visually and audibly unmistakable, with a persistent frame and colour that simulation never uses;
  \item real actions require signing on the person's own device, and the game never holds real keys;
  \item data produced by simulation is tagged as simulated, as Chapter 3 describes;
  \item game mechanics such as scores and rewards never appear in reality mode.
\end{itemize}
Interface improvements discovered in Oasis are adopted in the real Node only through Layer 5. Each Node may adapt the interface's language, metaphors, and appearance to its own culture while keeping the underlying protocols compatible.
